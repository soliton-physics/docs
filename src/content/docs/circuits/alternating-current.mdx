The source on this page swings its voltage back and forth as a sine wave. The first four circuits are shown in
their steady state, slowed so that one cycle takes about four seconds. The playground's graph has two views:
the voltage and current as waves, each drawn against its own peak so that the lag between them shows, and the
same quantities as rotating arrows, phasors.

## A resistor on alternating current

<Scenario id="ac-resistor" circuits height={440}>
<div slot="intuition">
A resistor does not care which way the current goes. At every instant the current is the voltage divided by the
resistance, so the two waves rise, fall and reverse together. Heat is produced whichever way the charge flows.
The average power is half the peak power, which is why AC is quoted by its RMS values: a 220 V supply has a
peak of 311 V and heats a resistor exactly as 220 V of direct current would.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
v = V_0\sin\omega t, \quad i = \frac{V_0}{R}\sin\omega t, \qquad
V_\text{rms} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt2}, \qquad \bar P = V_\text{rms}I_\text{rms} = \tfrac12 V_0I_0 .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Solved with complex numbers: each voltage and current is a complex amplitude, and a resistor's are in the ratio
$R$, a real number, so there is no phase between them.
</div>
</Scenario>

## An inductor on alternating current

<Scenario id="ac-inductor" circuits height={440}>
<div slot="intuition">
A coil's voltage depends on how fast the current is changing, not on how big it is. The current is changing
fastest as it passes through zero, so that is when the voltage is at its peak. The current reaches its own peak
a quarter of a cycle later. Over a cycle the coil takes energy in as its magnetic field builds and gives all of
it back as the field collapses, so on average it uses no power at all. The faster the alternation, the more the
coil opposes it.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
X_L = \omega L = 2\pi fL, \qquad I_0 = \frac{V_0}{X_L}, \qquad \text{current lags voltage by } 90°, \qquad \bar P = 0 .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The inductor's amplitudes are in the ratio $i\omega L$, an imaginary number: that is the quarter cycle. The
average power comes out zero to round-off.
</div>
</Scenario>

## A capacitor on alternating current

<Scenario id="ac-capacitor" circuits height={440}>
<div slot="intuition">
No charge crosses the gap in a capacitor, yet current flows in the wires all the time, as the plates are charged
one way and then the other. The current is largest when the voltage is changing fastest, as it passes through
zero, and is zero for an instant when the capacitor is full. So here the current reaches its peak a quarter of a
cycle before the voltage. A capacitor blocks direct current completely and passes alternating current more
easily the higher its frequency: the opposite of a coil.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{2\pi fC}, \qquad I_0 = \frac{V_0}{X_C}, \qquad \text{current leads voltage by } 90°, \qquad \bar P = 0 .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The lag is reported as −90°: a negative lag is a lead.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The series LCR circuit

<Scenario id="series-lcr" circuits height={440}>
<div slot="intuition">
All three in one loop, carrying the same current. The coil's voltage is a quarter cycle ahead of that current
and the capacitor's a quarter cycle behind, so the two are always opposite and partly cancel. Whichever is
larger decides whether the current lags or leads the source.

Now change the frequency. Raising it strengthens the coil's opposition and weakens the capacitor's. At one
frequency the two are equal and cancel completely. The source then sees only the resistor, and the current is
as large as it can be. Switch the graph to phasors there: the arrows for L and C are equal and opposite, and
each may be several times longer than the source's. That is resonance. Tuning a radio is choosing which
frequency a circuit like this resonates at.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{X_L - X_C}{R}, \qquad \bar P = V_\text{rms}I_\text{rms}\cos\varphi
$$

$$
f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad Q = \frac{\omega_0L}{R} .
$$

With 20 Ω, 200 mH and 100 μF the resonant frequency is 35.6 Hz.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The square root in $Z$ is never worked out by the engine: it solves the complex equations and the impedance is
what the source's voltage over the current comes to. It matches the formula to nine figures at 10, 50 and
200 Hz. The same circuit switched on and followed step by step, until the start has died away, settles to the
same amplitude to a part in ten thousand.
</div>
</Scenario>

## LC oscillations

<Scenario id="lc-oscillations" circuits height={440}>
<div slot="intuition">
A charged capacitor connected to a coil, and no resistance. The capacitor starts to empty through the coil. The
coil resists the growing current, then, once the capacitor is empty, resists its dying away: it keeps the
current going and charges the capacitor up the other way round. Then the whole thing runs back. Energy passes
between the capacitor's electric field and the coil's magnetic field, exactly as it passes between a spring and
a moving mass. With no resistance it would go on for ever.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}, \qquad I_0 = V_0\sqrt{\frac CL}, \qquad \tfrac12CV^2 + \tfrac12LI^2 = \text{constant} .
$$

The charge on the capacitor plays the part of displacement, the current that of velocity, $L$ that of mass and
$1/C$ that of the spring constant.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
This one is followed in time, not solved with complex numbers. After five whole periods the energy is unchanged
to nine figures and the capacitor is back at its starting voltage to a part in ten thousand.
</div>
</Scenario>
