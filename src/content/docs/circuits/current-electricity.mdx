## Ohm's law

<Scenario id="ohms-law" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
A battery keeps one of its terminals at a higher potential than the other, and charge flows round the circuit
from high to low like water downhill. The resistor is the narrow part of the pipe. Push harder, with a bigger
voltage, and more flows. Narrow the pipe, with a bigger resistance, and less does. The ammeter sits in the path
and counts what goes through; the voltmeter is connected across the resistor and compares the potential at its
two ends.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
V = IR, \qquad P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R} .
$$

Six volts across 12 Ω drives half an ampere and turns 3 W into heat.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
One equation for the cell, one for the resistor, and the current into each junction set to zero. The ammeter is
a wire that reports its current and the voltmeter a gap that reports its voltage.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Resistors in series

<Scenario id="resistors-in-series" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
There is only one way round, so whatever current goes through the first resistor goes through all of them. Each
takes a share of the battery's voltage to push that current through itself, and the largest resistance needs the
largest share. Make one resistor much bigger than the others and it takes nearly all of the voltage.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
R = R_1 + R_2 + R_3, \qquad I = \frac{V}{R}, \qquad V_k = IR_k .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Nobody adds the resistances. The three voltages are found separately and come out adding up to the battery's,
to round-off.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Resistors in parallel

<Scenario id="resistors-in-parallel" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
Now there are three ways round, and each resistor is connected straight across the battery, so each has the
whole voltage. The current divides at the junction, most of it taking the easiest path. Watch the dots: they
crowd down the smallest resistance. Adding a path always lets more current through in total, so the combination
has less resistance than its smallest member.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\frac1R = \frac1{R_1} + \frac1{R_2} + \frac1{R_3}, \qquad I_k = \frac{V}{R_k}, \qquad I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The junction rule is the equation here: the ammeter's current equals the three branch currents added, to
round-off.
</div>
</Scenario>

## A cell with internal resistance

<Scenario id="internal-resistance" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
A real cell is not a perfect source. Charge has to get through the cell itself, and that costs some of the EMF.
With nothing connected the voltmeter reads the full EMF. Connect a resistance and the reading drops; make the
resistance small and it drops a lot, while the cell warms up. Lower the load in the parameters and watch the
terminal voltage sag. The cell hands over the most power when the load equals its own internal resistance.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r}, \qquad V = \varepsilon - Ir, \qquad P_\text{max} = \frac{\varepsilon^2}{4r} \ \text{at}\ R = r .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The cell is an EMF in series with a resistance, as one component. Its current reads negative while it supplies,
by the sign rule on the first of these pages.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Two cells in parallel

<Scenario id="cells-in-parallel" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
Two different cells side by side feeding one resistance. Together they behave as a single cell, with an EMF
between the two and a smaller internal resistance than either. Now make the first cell much weaker than the
second. The strong cell pushes current backwards through the weak one: its dots reverse. That is charging, and it
is why mismatched cells should not be left connected in parallel.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\varepsilon_\text{eq} = \frac{\varepsilon_1 r_2 + \varepsilon_2 r_1}{r_1 + r_2}, \qquad
r_\text{eq} = \frac{r_1 r_2}{r_1 + r_2} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
With a 0.5 V cell beside a 6 V one the engine gives the weak cell a negative supplied current, as it should.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Kirchhoff's rules

<Scenario id="kirchhoff-rules" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
Two cells in different branches. Try to call anything here "in series" or "in parallel" and it does not work.
Two rules always do. At a junction, the currents in equal the currents out, because charge does not pile up.
Round any closed loop, the rises in potential equal the falls, because you end where you began. Write one
equation for the junction and one for each loop, and solve.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\sum I = 0 \ \text{at a junction}, \qquad \sum \Delta V = 0 \ \text{round a loop}.
$$

Calling the potential of the top junction $V$:

$$
\frac{\varepsilon_1 - V}{R_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2 - V}{R_2} = \frac{V}{R_3} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
This is what the engine does for every circuit on these pages; here it is also the subject. If a current comes
out negative, it flows against the direction it is named for: raise the right-hand cell and watch one reverse.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The Wheatstone bridge

<Scenario id="wheatstone-bridge" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
Current from the battery splits at the left corner, goes two ways round the diamond, and joins again at the
right. The galvanometer bridges the middle. If the top junction is at a higher potential than the bottom one,
current flows down through it; if lower, up. Change S in the parameters until the galvanometer reads zero. At
that point P/Q = R/S, and it stays zero whatever you do to the battery. That is what makes the bridge a precise
way to compare resistances: it depends on a zero, not on a reading.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Balanced when

$$
\frac PQ = \frac RS \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = \frac{QR}{P} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
At $S = QR/P$ the galvanometer's current is zero to round-off. Off balance it matches the full formula,
found by treating the rest of the bridge as a cell behind a resistance.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The metre bridge

<Scenario id="meter-bridge" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
The same bridge, built so that it can be balanced by hand. Two of its arms are the two parts of one metre of
uniform wire, the thick line. Drag the jockey along it. To one side the galvanometer reads one way, to the other
side the other way, and at one point it reads zero. The resistances of the two parts of the wire are in the ratio
of their lengths, so a ruler gives the unknown resistance.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With the balance point $l$ cm from the end next to the known resistance $R$:

$$
\frac{R}{S} = \frac{l}{100 - l} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = R\,\frac{100 - l}{l} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The wire is two resistors whose values follow the jockey. The reading is zero at $100R/(R+S)$ cm and of opposite
signs either side of it.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The potentiometer

<Scenario id="potentiometer" circuits>
<div slot="intuition">
A voltmeter draws a little current, so it never quite reads a cell's true EMF. The potentiometer does. A steady
current from the driver cell makes the potential fall evenly along the long wire. The cell being measured is
connected so that it opposes that fall, through a galvanometer and the jockey. Slide the jockey until the fall
along the wire up to it exactly matches the cell. Then no current flows through the cell at all, nothing is lost
inside it, and the length of wire is a measure of its EMF.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With a fall of $k$ volts per centimetre along the wire, balance at length $l$ means

$$
\varepsilon = kl, \qquad \frac{\varepsilon_1}{\varepsilon_2} = \frac{l_1}{l_2} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The test cell has an internal resistance of 1 Ω, and at balance it makes no difference: the reading is zero at
$100\,\varepsilon/V$ cm to round-off. Off balance the current matches the formula.
</div>
</Scenario>
