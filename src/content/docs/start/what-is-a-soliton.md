---
title: What is a soliton?
description: The one-paragraph version, the picture, and why it matters.
---

Drop a pebble in a pond and the ripples spread out and fade. Waves normally do that. Different wavelengths travel
at different speeds, so a bump made of many wavelengths pulls itself apart. That spreading is called **dispersion**.

Now watch a wave in a shallow canal, as John Scott Russell did on horseback in 1834. He saw a single hump of water
roll along for miles without spreading or breaking. He called it the "wave of translation". We call it a **soliton**.

## The balance

A soliton exists because two effects that would each destroy a wave happen to cancel:

- **Dispersion** makes the wave spread out.
- **Nonlinearity** makes the taller parts of the wave move faster, so the front steepens.

Spreading pulls the hump apart. Steepening pushes it together. For one special shape, the two are in perfect balance
at every point, and the hump moves forever without changing.

## Why physicists care

Solitons are not just a pond curiosity:

- Light pulses in optical fibres are solitons. They are why long-distance internet cables work.
- Bose–Einstein condensates host matter-wave solitons.
- Plasmas, the atmosphere, deep ocean currents and even protein chains carry them.
- Mathematically they sit at the heart of integrable systems, one of the most beautiful corners of physics.

The most surprising property: when two solitons collide, they pass through each other and come out unchanged,
except for a small shift in position. Particles do that. Waves are not supposed to. That is why the name ends in "-on",
like electron or photon.
