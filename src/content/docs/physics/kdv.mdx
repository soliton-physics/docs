## Intuition

Shallow water has a rule: taller waves move faster. A hump therefore steepens at its front, like a wave about to break
on a beach. But shallow water also has dispersion, which spreads a hump out. The Korteweg–de Vries equation is the
simplest model that contains both effects, and it has an exact hump shape where they cancel perfectly.

<LiveSim preset="kdv-soliton" />

Notice the shape never changes and the speed is constant. Taller solitons are narrower and faster.
That is not a coincidence. Height, width and speed are locked together by one parameter.

Now watch two of them meet.

<LiveSim preset="kdv-collision" />

The fast one catches the slow one, they merge into a single hump for a moment, and then both reappear exactly as
before. The only trace of the collision is that the fast soliton is a little ahead of where it would have been, and the
slow one is a little behind.

What if you start with a shape that is not a soliton?

<LiveSim preset="kdv-fission" />

The bump sorts itself into a line of solitons, tallest first, leaving behind a small ripple that spreads away.
The number and heights of the solitons that come out are determined by the initial shape alone. That is the
inverse scattering transform at work.
</div>

## The mathematics

The equation, in the normalisation this engine uses, is

$$
u_t + 6\,u\,u_x + u_{xxx} = 0 .
$$

The term $6 u u_x$ is the nonlinear steepening and $u_{xxx}$ is the dispersion. Look for a travelling wave
$u(x,t) = f(x - ct)$ and you find the single-soliton solution

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{c}{2}\,\operatorname{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{c}}{2}\,(x - ct - x_0)\right).
$$

One parameter, the speed $c$, fixes the amplitude $c/2$ and the width $2/\sqrt{c}$.

The equation has infinitely many conserved quantities. The engine tracks the first three:

| Name | Expression |
| --- | --- |
| Mass | $\int u \, dx$ |
| Momentum | $\int u^2 \, dx$ |
| Energy | $\int \left( u^3 - \tfrac{1}{2} u_x^2 \right) dx$ |

The energy is the Hamiltonian that generates the flow, $u_t = -\partial_x\, \delta H / \delta u$.
</div>

## The numerics

On a periodic domain the engine writes the equation as $u_t = L u + N(u)$ with

$$
L(k) = i k^3, \qquad N(u) = -3\,(u^2)_x .
$$

In Fourier space $L$ is just a number for each wavenumber, so the linear part is handled exactly. The nonlinear part is
computed in physical space and transformed back. This is the [pseudo-spectral method](/docs/numerics/spectral-methods/).
Time stepping uses [ETDRK4](/docs/numerics/etdrk4/), which treats the stiff dispersive term exactly and the nonlinear
term to fourth order.
