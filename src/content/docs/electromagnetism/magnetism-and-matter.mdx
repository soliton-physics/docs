On this page the magnetic field lies in the page, the way it does round a magnet lying on a table. A magnet is
painted red at its north pole and blue at its south, and the small needles all over the stage are compasses: the
red end of each points the way the field goes.

## Field of a bar magnet

<Scenario id="bar-magnet-field">
<div slot="intuition">
The lines leave the north pole, loop round, and come back in at the south. They never start or stop in empty
space, because nobody has ever found a north pole without its south. Drag the compass along the axis, out past the
north pole, and read the field. Now carry it to the same distance beside the middle of the magnet. The field there
points the opposite way and is half as strong.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
For a magnet of moment $m$ and length $2l$, at distance $r$ from its centre:

$$
B_\text{axis} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2mr}{(r^2 - l^2)^2}, \qquad
B_\text{beside} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{m}{(r^2 + l^2)^{3/2}}.
$$

Far away, $r \gg l$, these become $\mu_0 2m/4\pi r^3$ and $\mu_0 m/4\pi r^3$: the field of a dipole, the same
shape as the electric field of two opposite charges.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
A magnet is modelled as two poles, north and south, at its ends, each acting by an inverse-square law. Outside the
magnet that gives exactly the two formulas above; the test checks them to round-off. Inside the magnet the true
field runs from south to north and the model's does not, so nothing is read there. The compass is a small magnet
on a pivot, set down already at rest: it lies along the field averaged over its own length, which near the magnet
is a few degrees from the field at its middle.
</div>
</Scenario>

## A compass needle set swinging

<Scenario id="compass-needle">
<div slot="intuition">
A uniform field cannot pull a magnet anywhere, since it pulls the two poles equally in opposite directions. What it
can do is twist. Turn the needle away from the field and let go: it swings back, overshoots, and keeps swinging.
Make the field stronger and it swings faster. This is how a magnetic field is measured with nothing but a needle
and a stopwatch.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\tau = mB\sin\theta, \qquad U = -mB\cos\theta .
$$

For small angles $\tau \approx -mB\theta$, which is simple harmonic motion:

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mB}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B = \frac{4\pi^2 I}{mT^2}.
$$

Let go from $\theta_0$, it passes through the field direction turning at
$\omega = \sqrt{2mB(1 - \cos\theta_0)/I}$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
No torque is put in by hand: the field pushes on each pole and the twist follows. Released at 5° the period
matches the formula to two parts in a thousand; at 20° it reads about 1 % long, as any pendulum does. Released at
60° the fastest spin matches the energy equation to a part in ten thousand, and the needle's centre does not move.
</div>
</Scenario>

## A bar magnet in the earth's field

<Scenario id="neutral-point">
<div slot="intuition">
A magnet on a table is never alone: the earth's field is there too, pointing north, up the page. The two add.
With the magnet's north pole pointing north, its own field beside it points south, against the earth's. At one
distance either side the two are equal and cancel: a neutral point. The lattice of needles fades out there. Carry
the compass to it and watch the reading drop to nothing. Then turn the magnet round, and look for the neutral
points on its axis.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
North pole pointing north, neutral points east and west at distance $d$:

$$
\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{m}{(d^2 + l^2)^{3/2}} = B_H .
$$

North pole pointing south, neutral points on the axis:

$$
\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2md}{(d^2 - l^2)^2} = B_H .
$$

Either one, measured with a ruler, gives the moment of the magnet if the earth's field is known.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The earth's field is a uniform background of the size you choose; about 35 μT is its horizontal part over much of
India. With the compass placed at the predicted point, the field on it is zero to a part in a billion of the
earth's. The compass is itself a weak magnet, so the pointer readout a few centimetres from it includes a little of
its field.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Pushed out or pulled in

<Scenario id="magnetic-materials">
<div slot="intuition">
Every material responds to a magnet, most of them far too feebly to notice. A sample of bismuth is magnetised
against the field it sits in, and is pushed gently away from the pole, towards where the field is weaker: it is
diamagnetic. Slide the susceptibility up past zero to aluminium's value and the sample is magnetised along the
field and drifts in: paramagnetic. The forces are millionths of a newton next to a strong magnet. Iron's response
is thousands of times larger and does not follow this simple rule.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
A sample of volume $V$ and susceptibility $\chi$ takes on a moment $m = \chi V B/\mu_0$ along the field for
$\chi > 0$ and against it for $\chi < 0$. In a field that changes from place to place the force on it is

$$
F = \frac{\chi V}{\mu_0}\, B\,\frac{dB}{dx} = \frac{\chi V}{2\mu_0}\,\frac{d(B^2)}{dx} .
$$

Positive $\chi$: towards stronger field. Negative: towards weaker. Bismuth has $\chi = -1.66\times10^{-4}$,
aluminium $+2.2\times10^{-5}$, water $-9\times10^{-6}$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The sample is a one cubic centimetre ball of mass 10 g, hanging where gravity does not matter. The engine
magnetises it in proportion to the field at its centre and applies the force above, with the equal and opposite
force on the magnet. The force at the start matches the formula to a part in a million, and kinetic plus magnetic
energy holds to a part in a million as it drifts. Ferromagnets, with their saturation and memory, are not
modelled.
</div>
</Scenario>
