## Displacement current

<Wave id="displacement-current" height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
Charge flows along the wire onto one plate and off the other, but nothing crosses the gap. So is there a current
there or not? Ampère's law needs an answer, because a magnetic field circles the wire and it would be strange for
it to stop dead at the plates. Maxwell's answer: in the gap there is an electric field that is growing, and a
growing electric field makes a magnetic field just as a current does. He called it the displacement current.

Watch the two readings in the panel. The current in the wires and the displacement current in the gap are equal
at every moment, falling together as the capacitor fills. The graph is one curve drawn twice.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Charging through a resistance $R$:

$$
q = CV\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right), \qquad i_c = \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{V}{R}\,e^{-t/RC} .
$$

Between the plates $E = q/\varepsilon_0 A$, so

$$
i_d = \varepsilon_0\,\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \varepsilon_0 A\,\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{dq}{dt} = i_c ,
$$

and Ampère's law becomes

$$
\oint \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0\left(i_c + \varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\right).
$$
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Click the graph early and late and compare the field between the plates with the current. The field is largest
  when the current has nearly stopped: it is the rate of change of the field that counts, not the field.
- Make the plates bigger. The capacitance and the time constant go up, and the magnetic field at the rim goes down,
  because the same current is spread round a longer circle.
- The magnetic field reads in picoteslas. That is why nobody noticed it before Maxwell predicted it.
</div>
</Wave>

## An electromagnetic wave

<Wave id="electromagnetic-wave">
<div slot="intuition">
Put the two halves together. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field: that is Faraday. A changing
electric field makes a magnetic field: that is Maxwell's new term. So each can keep the other going, and the pair
travels through empty space with nothing to carry it.

The two fields are at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, and they are in step: both
largest at the same place and time, both zero together. Point your fingers along E, curl them towards B, and your
thumb points the way the wave goes.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
E_y = E_0\sin(kx - \omega t), \qquad B_z = B_0\sin(kx - \omega t), \qquad \frac{E_0}{B_0} = c .
$$

The speed comes out of two constants measured on a laboratory bench:

$$
c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}, \qquad v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{n}\ \text{in a medium}.
$$

The wave carries energy $I = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 c E_0^2$ across each square metre every second, and momentum
$U/c$ with it.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Click a crest. The electric field reads 120 V/m and the magnetic field 400 nT. The second number looks tiny only
  because of the units: the two carry equal energy.
- Switch from empty space to glass. The frequency cannot change, so the wavelength shrinks to two thirds.
- The clock says how much the wave has been slowed to be watched: three hundred million times.
</div>
</Wave>

## Radio waves off a metal sheet

<Wave id="electromagnetic-standing-wave">
<div slot="intuition">
This is how Hertz showed, in 1887, that Maxwell's waves are real. A metal sheet reflects the wave, and the wave
going out and the wave coming back make a standing pattern. He walked a small wire loop along it and found places
where it sparked and places where it did not. The dead places are half a wavelength apart, so a ruler gives the
wavelength, and with the frequency of his circuit, the speed. It was the speed of light.

At the metal the electric field has to be zero, because a conductor will not stand a field along its surface. The
magnetic field is largest there. In a standing wave, unlike a travelling one, the two are out of step.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With the sheet at $x = L$:

$$
E_y = 2E_0\sin k(x - L)\cos\omega t, \qquad B_z = -\frac{2E_0}{c}\cos k(x - L)\sin\omega t .
$$

Nodes of $E$ at the sheet and every $\lambda/2$ from it, and $c = f\lambda = f\times 2\times(\text{node to node})$.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Click a node of the electric field, marked N. The magnetic field there swings through its largest values.
- Watch one moment when the electric field is zero everywhere. The magnetic field is at its largest: the energy is
  all magnetic then, and all electric a quarter of a period later.
- Raise the frequency and count the nodes in the 12 m.
</div>
</Wave>

## The electromagnetic spectrum

<Wave id="electromagnetic-spectrum">
<div slot="intuition">
Radio, microwaves, the warmth of a fire, light, the rays that tan skin, X-rays and gamma rays are one kind of wave.
They differ in frequency and in nothing else, and in empty space they all travel at the same speed. The slider
runs through eighteen powers of ten. The wave on the graph always looks the same; it is the unit on the axis that
changes, from kilometres to picometres. The strip underneath shows where you are. Visible light is the thin
coloured sliver.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
c = f\lambda, \qquad E_\text{photon} = hf .
$$

| Band | Wavelength | Made by |
|---|---|---|
| Radio | more than 0.1 m | charges driven along an aerial |
| Microwaves | 0.1 m to 1 mm | klystrons, magnetrons |
| Infrared | 1 mm to 700 nm | warm bodies, vibrating molecules |
| Visible | 700 nm to 400 nm | electrons changing level in atoms |
| Ultraviolet | 400 nm to 1 nm | very hot bodies, special lamps |
| X-rays | 1 nm to 0.001 nm | fast electrons stopped by a target |
| Gamma rays | less than 0.001 nm | nuclei |

The edges are conventions and neighbouring bands overlap.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Find the frequency of a mobile phone, about 10⁹ Hz, and read the wavelength: about the size of the phone.
- Slide through the visible band and watch the wavelength go from about 750 nm to 390 nm.
- At what frequency is the wavelength the size of an atom, 0.1 nm? Which band is that, and why are those rays
  used to study crystals?
</div>
</Wave>
