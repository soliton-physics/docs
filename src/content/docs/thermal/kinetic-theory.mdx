## The pressure of a gas

<Scenario id="ideal-gas" thermal>
<div slot="intuition">
A gas pushes on its container because its molecules keep hitting the walls. Each blow is tiny, but there are a
great many of them, and together they make a steady push. Open the parameters and change one thing at a time.
Twice as many molecules, twice as many blows. A hotter gas has faster molecules, which hit harder and more often.
A smaller box, and each molecule comes round to a wall sooner.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
A molecule of mass $m$ bouncing off a wall changes its momentum by $2mv_x$. Adding up the blows of $N$ molecules
on a wall gives

$$
P = \frac13 \frac{N}{V}\, m\,\overline{v^2}, \qquad \tfrac12 m\,\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 kT
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad PV = NkT .
$$

With 300 argon atoms at 300 K in a box 12 nm across, $P = 7.1$ atm and $v_\text{rms} = \sqrt{3kT/m} = 433$ m/s.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The pressure shown is not worked out from the temperature. The engine adds up the momentum the molecules hand to
the six walls and divides by the time and the area. Averaged over a nanosecond it agrees with $NkT/V$ to within
4 % for every combination tested, a per cent or two of which is real: the molecules have a size. The temperature
of an insulated gas does not change at all, to twelve figures, because every collision conserves energy exactly.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The Maxwell distribution of speeds

<Scenario id="maxwell-distribution" thermal>
<div slot="intuition">
At the start every molecule has exactly the same speed, in a random direction: the bars are a single spike. Watch
what a few collisions do. A glancing blow leaves one molecule faster and the other slower, and soon there are
molecules at every speed. The bars settle onto the curve and stay there.

Nothing steers them to it. The curve is simply the way of sharing out the energy that can be done in the most
ways, so it is the one you almost always find.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
f(v) = 4\pi \left(\frac{m}{2\pi kT}\right)^{3/2} v^2\, e^{-mv^2/2kT}
$$

$$
v_\text{most probable} = \sqrt{\frac{2kT}{m}}, \qquad \bar v = \sqrt{\frac{8kT}{\pi m}}, \qquad v_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} .
$$

The three are in the ratio $1 : 1.13 : 1.22$. Heavier molecules, or a colder gas, and the whole curve moves to the
left and grows taller.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The root mean square speed cannot change, because the total energy cannot. The mean speed starts equal to it and
falls to $0.921$ of it as the distribution forms; the measured value matches $\sqrt{8kT/\pi m}$ to within 2 %.
The bars are smoothed over about a second of watching, because 400 molecules sorted into 28 bins are a rough
sample.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Light and heavy molecules together

<Scenario id="gas-mixture" thermal>
<div slot="intuition">
Neon at 600 K and xenon at 200 K, mixed in one box. The neon atoms are light and fast, the xenon heavy and slow.
In each collision a little energy passes from the more energetic to the less, and the two graphs of temperature
close in on each other. They meet when a neon atom and a xenon atom have the same kinetic energy on average. That
is what "the same temperature" means. It is not the same speed: xenon is six and a half times heavier, and ends
up moving about two and a half times more slowly.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Equipartition: each kind of molecule has $\tfrac12 m\,\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 kT$, so

$$
\frac{v_\text{rms, light}}{v_\text{rms, heavy}} = \sqrt{\frac{m_\text{heavy}}{m_\text{light}}} .
$$

With equal numbers of each, the temperature they settle at is the average of the two they began with.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The final temperature of the whole mixture is exact, since no energy leaves. The temperature of each gas alone,
averaged after 600 ps, is within 6 % of it, and each root mean square speed within 4 %.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Brownian motion

<Scenario id="brownian-motion" thermal>
<div slot="intuition">
The big blue sphere is a grain fifty times heavier than the atoms around it. At any instant it is being hit from
all sides, and the blows very nearly cancel. Not quite: now a few more from the left, now from below. So it
jitters and wanders, and the line shows where it has been.

Robert Brown saw pollen grains doing this in water in 1827 and nobody could explain it. Einstein's explanation in
1905, and Perrin's measurements, are what finally convinced physicists that atoms are real.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The grain is one more particle in the gas and has the same share of energy as any atom:

$$
\tfrac12 M\,\overline{V^2} = \tfrac32 kT, \qquad V_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{M}} .
$$

A grain of 2000 u at 300 K moves at about 61 m/s, against 433 m/s for the argon.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The grain starts at rest and is brought to the temperature of the gas by collisions alone. One particle is a very
small sample, so its measured temperature settles slowly and is counted as agreeing within 25 %. Leave it running:
the average creeps towards the temperature of the gas.
</div>
</Scenario>
