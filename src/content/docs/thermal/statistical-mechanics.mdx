This page goes beyond the school syllabus, into the methods of a university course in statistical and
computational physics. The four experiments have one thing in common: nothing in them is followed by Newton's
laws. Each is run by chance, with random numbers, and the physics is in the averages. They are in the Thermal tab
of the playground, after the gas.

## Finding π by throwing darts

<Scenario id="monte-carlo-pi" thermal>
<div slot="intuition">
Draw a square, and inside it a quarter of a circle that just fits. Throw points at the square without aiming. The
chance that a point lands inside the curve is the ratio of the two areas, $\pi/4$. So count: four times the
fraction inside is an estimate of $\pi$.

Watch how it converges. It is close after a few hundred points and then improves painfully slowly. The dashed
band on the graph is the error statistics predicts, and it narrows only as one over the square root of the
number of points. A hundred times the work buys one more decimal place. That is the price of every Monte Carlo
method, and its strength is that the price is the same for a problem in a hundred dimensions as in two.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With $N$ points and a fraction $p = \pi/4$ inside:

$$
\pi \approx 4\,\frac{N_\text{in}}{N}, \qquad \sigma = 4\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}} \approx \frac{1.64}{\sqrt N} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
After 400 000 points the estimate is within three of those standard deviations of $\pi$. The random numbers come
from a seeded generator, so a run can be repeated exactly.
</div>
</Scenario>

## A crowd of random walkers

<Scenario id="random-walk" thermal>
<div slot="intuition">
Two thousand walkers start together. At every tick each takes one step, north, south, east or west, chosen at
random. Follow any one and it wanders aimlessly, as likely to come back as to go on. But the crowd as a whole
spreads in a perfectly orderly way. The dashed circle is at a distance of the square root of the number of steps,
and the crowd fills it. Four times as long to spread twice as far.

This is what diffusion is: ink in water, heat along a rod, the Brownian grain on the kinetic theory page. Nothing
drives the spreading. It is only that there are more ways to be spread out than bunched up.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
After $N$ steps of unit length the displacement has zero mean and

$$
\langle r^2 \rangle = N, \qquad r_\text{rms} = \sqrt N .
$$

In time $t$ with steps of length $a$ every $\tau$ seconds this is $\langle r^2\rangle = 4Dt$ with
$D = a^2/4\tau$, the diffusion law in two dimensions.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
With 4000 walkers the root mean square distance is within 5 % of $\sqrt N$ at 100, 400 and 1600 steps, and the
middle of the crowd stays where it began to within what that many walkers allow.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The Ising model of a magnet

<Scenario id="ising-model" thermal height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
Each little square is the spin of one atom, pointing up or down. A spin has lower energy when it agrees with its
four neighbours, so left to themselves they would all line up: a magnet. Heat works against that, flipping spins
at random.

Open the parameters and move the temperature. Well below 2.27 the lattice is nearly all one colour, with a few
spins knocked out of line. Well above, it is a fine-grained flicker with no overall magnetisation. Close to 2.27
it is neither: patches of every size, forming and dissolving, slow to make up its mind. That is a system at a
critical point. Add a field and the spins favour its direction even when hot.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
E = -J \sum_{\langle ij \rangle} s_i s_j - h \sum_i s_i, \qquad s_i = \pm 1 .
$$

Onsager solved the square lattice exactly in 1944, for no field:

$$
kT_c = \frac{2J}{\ln(1 + \sqrt 2)} = 2.269\,J, \qquad
m = \left[1 - \sinh^{-4}\frac{2J}{kT}\right]^{1/8} \ (T < T_c) .
$$

The Metropolis rule: pick a spin and propose to flip it. If that lowers the energy, flip it. If it raises the
energy by $\Delta E$, flip it with probability $e^{-\Delta E/kT}$. Repeated, this visits states in proportion to
their Boltzmann weights.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
At temperatures of 1.5, 2.0, 3.0 and 4.0 the averaged energy matches Onsager's to within 0.06 J and the
magnetisation to within 0.04 below the critical temperature. Above it a lattice of 48 by 48 keeps a
magnetisation of a few hundredths where an infinite one has none, and near it the two differ by more: a finite
lattice has no sharp transition. Temperatures are in units of $J/k$.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Cooling a magnet through its critical point

<Scenario id="phase-transition" thermal height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
Now the experiment a laboratory would do. The lattice starts hot and disordered and is cooled in 25 steps. At
each temperature it is given time to settle and is then measured. Watch the graph fill in from the right.

For a long while nothing happens: the magnetisation stays near zero. Then, over a narrow range of temperature, it
rises steeply and levels off near one. The dashed curve is Onsager's exact answer, which turns on instantly at
the critical temperature; the lattice follows it everywhere but in the rounded corner. Switch the graph to
specific heat and there is a peak at the same place: just there, a little heat goes a long way in rearranging
the spins.

Nobody put a transition into the program. There are only spins, their neighbours, and a rule for flipping.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The specific heat and the susceptibility come from fluctuations, with $N$ spins:

$$
c = \frac{N}{kT^2}\left(\langle e^2 \rangle - \langle e \rangle^2\right), \qquad
\chi = \frac{N}{kT}\left(\langle m^2 \rangle - \langle |m| \rangle^2\right) .
$$

Near $T_c$ the magnetisation of an infinite lattice vanishes as $(T_c - T)^{1/8}$, and the specific heat
diverges logarithmically.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
With 32 by 32 spins, 200 sweeps to settle and 400 to measure at each temperature, the specific heat peaks within
0.2 of the critical temperature, and away from it every point is within 0.06 J of Onsager's energy. A larger
lattice sharpens the corner and takes longer; near the critical point the Metropolis rule itself slows down, a
real effect called critical slowing down.
</div>
</Scenario>
