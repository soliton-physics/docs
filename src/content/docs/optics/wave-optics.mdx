Ray optics treats light as straight lines. That works until the light meets something about as small as its own
wavelength; then it spreads, overlaps and cancels as only a wave can. These three are in the Waves part of the
playground. Each is a steady pattern: the graph is the brightness across a screen, and the band under it is what
the screen would look like, in the colour of the light chosen.

## Young's double slit

<Wave id="youngs-double-slit" height={440}>
<div slot="intuition">
Light from one source passes through two narrow slits close together, and the two beams spread and overlap on a
distant screen. You might expect a brighter patch. What appears is a row of evenly spaced bright and dark
stripes.

At the centre of the screen the two paths are equal, the waves arrive crest on crest, and it is bright. A little
to one side, one path is half a wavelength longer: crest meets trough, and there is no light at all. Further on
the difference is a whole wavelength and it is bright again. Light plus light can give darkness. Nothing but a
wave can do that, and in 1801 it settled the question of what light is.

The broad hump over the stripes is the pattern of one slit alone. Narrow the slits and it widens.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
At a point $y$ from the centre, with slits $d$ apart and the screen $D$ away, the paths differ by $yd/D$.

$$
\text{bright: } \frac{yd}{D} = n\lambda, \qquad \text{dark: } \frac{yd}{D} = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\lambda, \qquad
\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d} .
$$

$$
I = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\varphi}{2}, \qquad \varphi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,\frac{yd}{D} .
$$

With yellow light of 590 nm, slits 0.25 mm apart and a screen 1.5 m away, the fringes are 3.54 mm wide.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Slide the wavelength from red to violet. The stripes close up: violet fringes are a little over half the width
  of red ones.
- Click a bright fringe and then a dark one beside it. The path difference changes by exactly half a wavelength.
- Halve the distance between the slits. The fringes double in width. This is why the slits must be very close to
  give fringes wide enough to see.
</div>
</Wave>

## Diffraction at a single slit

<Wave id="single-slit-diffraction" height={440}>
<div slot="intuition">
One slit, and still not a sharp-edged patch of light. The middle is a broad bright band, twice as wide as you
would guess, with faint bands either side.

Think of the slit as a row of tiny sources side by side, each sending out a wavelet. Straight ahead they all
agree. At the angle where the wavelets from the two edges differ by one whole wavelength, each source in the top
half has a partner in the bottom half exactly half a wavelength out of step with it, and they cancel pair by
pair: a dark band.

Now make the slit narrower. The bright band gets wider. Squeezing light makes it spread, and that is the limit
on every lens, telescope and microscope.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
a\sin\theta = n\lambda \ \ (n = \pm1, \pm2, \dots) \ \text{for dark bands}, \qquad
\text{central width} = \frac{2\lambda D}{a} .
$$

$$
I = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^2, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda} .
$$

A beam of width $a$ stays a beam for about the Fresnel distance $a^2/\lambda$. Nearer than that, ray optics is a
good description.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Read the Fresnel distance for a 0.1 mm slit: under two centimetres. For a 3 mm hole it is about 15 m, which is
  why light through a window seems to travel in straight lines.
- Click the first side band. It has under a twentieth of the brightness of the middle.
- Compare with the double slit above: there, bright where the path difference is a whole wavelength; here, dark.
  Work out why both are right.
</div>
</Wave>

## Polarisation and Malus's law

<Wave id="malus-law" height={440}>
<div slot="intuition">
Sound cannot be polarised and light can, because light vibrates across its direction of travel and sound along
it. Ordinary light vibrates in all directions across its path at once. A polaroid passes only the vibration
along one axis, so it lets half the light through and what comes out vibrates in one direction only.

A second polaroid shows this. With its axis parallel to the first, all of that light passes. Turn it, and less
gets through. At a right angle, none: two transparent sheets together are black. Drag the angle and watch the
beam after the second polaroid thin out and vanish.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
I_1 = \tfrac12 I_0, \qquad I_2 = I_1\cos^2\theta .
$$

Only the component $E\cos\theta$ of the vibration along the second axis passes, and brightness goes as its
square.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Find the angle at which the second polaroid passes half of what it receives. It is 45°, not 60°.
- At 90° nothing passes. A third polaroid slipped between the two at 45° would let light through again: an eighth
  of the original. Check it with the formula, applied twice.
</div>
</Wave>
