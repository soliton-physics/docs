## Image in a concave mirror

<Scenario id="concave-mirror" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
Three rays from the top of the object are easy to follow. One parallel to the axis comes back through the focus.
One aimed at the centre of curvature hits the mirror square on and comes straight back. One aimed at the pole
leaves at the same angle on the other side of the axis. Where they meet is the top of the image.

Drag the object in from far away. The image moves out and grows; at the centre of curvature it is the same size;
between there and the focus it is enlarged. Cross the focus and the reflected rays no longer meet. They spread, as
though from a point behind the mirror: an upright, enlarged, virtual image, the one in a shaving mirror.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\frac1v + \frac1u = \frac1f, \qquad f = \frac R2, \qquad m = \frac{h'}{h} = -\frac vu .
$$

For a concave mirror $f$ is negative. With $u = -40$ cm and $f = -15$ cm, $v = -24$ cm: a real image 24 cm in
front of the mirror, and $m = -0.6$, upside down and smaller.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The mirror is an arc of a circle. Each ray is reflected about the normal at the point it strikes, which is the
radius there. The image distance and magnification traced from near-axis rays match the formula to a part in a
million wherever the object is. The red rays from a tall object cross a little short of the blue arrow's tip:
that gap is spherical aberration, not an error.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Image in a convex mirror

<Scenario id="convex-mirror" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
A convex mirror bulges towards you and spreads every ray that hits it. The reflected rays never meet in front.
Follow them backwards, behind the mirror, and they all point to one place between the pole and the focus. Wherever
you put the object, the image is there: upright, smaller, virtual. A small image of a wide scene is what a driver
wants from a wing mirror.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The same formula, with $f$ positive:

$$
\frac1v + \frac1u = \frac1f .
$$

With $u = -30$ cm and $f = +15$ cm, $v = +10$ cm and $m = -v/u = +\tfrac13$. As $u \to -\infty$, $v \to f$: the image
never gets further back than the focus.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Same mirror, facing the other way. The image is found by producing the last stretch of two near-axis rays
backwards until they cross; because they cross behind the point where the rays turned, it is reported as virtual
and drawn dashed.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Refraction at a flat surface

<Scenario id="snells-law" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
Light travels more slowly in water or glass than in air, and where its speed changes it bends. Going into the
slower medium it bends towards the normal; coming out, away from it. Drag the start of the ray round and watch
the two angles: they are never equal, but their sines keep the same ratio.

Now open the parameters and make the medium above the denser one. The ray bends away from the normal, and as
you raise the angle it gets closer and closer to skimming the surface. Past the critical angle it cannot get out.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r, \qquad n = \frac{c}{v} .
$$

From air into water at 45°: $\sin r = \sin 45°/1.33$, so $r = 32.1°$. From a denser medium into a rarer one there
is no $r$ once $\sin i > n_2/n_1$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Snell's law is applied in vector form, with the refractive index sampled a hair either side of the surface to
learn what the ray is passing between. The traced angle matches to round-off at every incidence from 0° to 89°.
Sent back along its own path, a ray retraces it exactly.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Through a glass slab

<Scenario id="glass-slab" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
A slab has two parallel faces. The ray is bent towards the normal going in and away from it by the same amount
coming out, so it leaves parallel to the way it arrived. The whole effect of the slab is to shift the ray
sideways. A thicker slab or a steeper ray shifts it more.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With $\sin i = n \sin r$, the ray emerges at $i$ again, displaced sideways by

$$
d = \frac{t\,\sin(i - r)}{\cos r} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Two refractions, each exact. The traced total turning is zero to round-off and the angle of emergence equals the
angle of incidence.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Apparent depth

<Scenario id="apparent-depth" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
Rays from a coin at the bottom spread as they rise, and spread more once they leave the water, because each is
bent away from the normal. An eye above follows them straight back, along the dashed lines, to a point above the
coin. The pool looks about three quarters as deep as it is.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
For rays close to the vertical,

$$
\text{apparent depth} = \frac{\text{real depth}}{n} .
$$

At 20 cm in water, $20/1.33 = 15$ cm: the coin appears raised by 5 cm.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The image is found from two rays a hair either side of the vertical, produced backwards. It matches $d/n$ to a
part in a million. The outer rays of the fan, traced back, cross a little higher still: seen at a slant the coin
looks shallower again.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Total internal reflection

<Scenario id="total-internal-reflection" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
A lamp under water sends rays up in all directions. The steep ones get out, bent towards the surface. As a ray
leans over, the one in the air leans faster, until it is skimming the surface. That is the critical angle. Any ray
flatter than that has nowhere to go in the air, and all of it is reflected: the surface becomes a perfect mirror.
From below, the world above is squeezed into a bright circle overhead.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\sin i_c = \frac{1}{n}, \qquad i_c = 48.8° \text{ for water}.
$$

A lamp at depth $h$ lights a circle of radius $h\tan i_c = h/\sqrt{n^2 - 1}$ on the surface.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Nothing switches this on. Snell's law asks for $\sin r > 1$, which no angle has, and the ray is reflected by the
ordinary law instead. A hundredth of a degree inside the critical angle the ray leaves at more than 88° to the
normal; a hundredth beyond it, it is turned back.
</div>
</Scenario>

## An optical fibre

<Scenario id="optical-fibre" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
A fibre is a thread of glass inside a coat of glass that is slightly less dense. Light sent in nearly along the
thread meets the wall at a glancing angle, well beyond the critical angle, and is totally reflected, again and
again, all the way along. Nothing leaks, even round a bend. Drag the ray so that it enters more steeply: once it
is outside the fibre's acceptance angle it meets the wall too squarely and escapes into the coat.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
At the wall, $\sin i_c = n_2/n_1$. Light entering the end at $\theta$ is carried if

$$
\sin\theta \le \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2} .
$$

With a core of 1.50 and a cladding of 1.40 the widest angle is 32.6°.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The core is a rectangle of glass inside a larger rectangle of cladding; where two pieces overlap, the inner one
is what is there. Inside the acceptance angle every meeting with the wall is a total reflection. Five degrees
outside it, the first meeting is a refraction into the cladding.
</div>
</Scenario>
