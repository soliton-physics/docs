## Refraction at a curved surface

<Scenario id="spherical-surface" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
Before a lens, one curved face. Rays from a point on the axis meet the glass at different places, and the normal
there, the radius, tilts more the further out they hit. So the outer rays are bent more, and all of them come
together at a point inside the glass. Bring the object closer and that point runs away; closer still and the rays
are spreading even after the surface, as though from a point behind the object.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
From a medium of index $n_1$ into one of $n_2$, through a surface of radius $R$:

$$
\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R} .
$$

From air into glass of 1.5 with $R = 10$ cm and $u = -40$ cm: $1.5/v = 0.05 - 0.025$, so $v = 60$ cm.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The glass is a long block with a spherical cap for a face. The image from near-axis rays matches the formula to a
part in a million. The outer rays of the fan cross the axis a little early.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Image formed by a convex lens

<Scenario id="convex-lens" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
Three rays from the top of the object. The one parallel to the axis is bent through the far focus. The one
through the centre of the lens goes straight on. The one that passes through the near focus comes out parallel.
They meet at the top of the image.

Drag the object in. Beyond 2F the image is smaller; at 2F the same size; between 2F and F larger, and further
away. At the focus the rays leave parallel and there is no image. Inside the focus they spread, and traced back
they give an upright, enlarged, virtual image on the same side as the object: a magnifying glass.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\frac1v - \frac1u = \frac1f, \qquad m = \frac vu, \qquad P = \frac1f \ \ (\text{dioptres, with } f \text{ in metres}).
$$

With $u = -40$ cm and $f = +15$ cm, $v = +24$ cm and $m = -0.6$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
This lens is ideal: a line that changes each ray's slope by its height over $f$. Every ray from a point, however
far from the axis, goes to the same image point, so the red rays meet exactly at the blue arrow. With the object
at the focus the two near-axis rays leave parallel, and the engine reports no image.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Image formed by a concave lens

<Scenario id="concave-lens" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
A concave lens is thinner in the middle and spreads light. The ray parallel to the axis leaves as though it had
come from the focus on the object's own side. The image is always there, between the lens and that focus:
upright, smaller, virtual. Lenses like this correct short sight.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The same formula with $f$ negative. With $u = -30$ cm and $f = -15$ cm, $v = -10$ cm and $m = +\tfrac13$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The same ideal lens with a negative focal length; the arrowheads on its symbol point inward.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The lens maker's formula

<Scenario id="lens-maker" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
Here the lens is real: glass, with two curved faces, and Snell's law at each. Make a face more curved and the
focus moves in. Use denser glass and it moves in again. That is all a lens maker has to work with.

Now widen the beam with the knob. The outer rays cross the axis short of where the inner ones do. A lens with
spherical faces has no single focus for a wide beam; the formula gives the focus for rays near the axis.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
For a lens in air with faces of radius $R_1$ and $R_2$, both bulging outward:

$$
\frac1f = (n - 1)\left(\frac1{R_1} + \frac1{R_2}\right).
$$

With both radii 30 cm and $n = 1.5$, $f = 30$ cm and the power is 3.33 D.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The lens is two spherical caps meeting at a sharp edge, 10 cm across and about 8 mm thick in the middle. Two
rays a fifth of a millimetre from the axis give a focus within about 1 % of the thin-lens formula; the small difference
is the thickness the formula leaves out. The second number is where the outermost rays of the beam cross, and it
falls short by more as the beam widens.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Two lenses in contact

<Scenario id="lenses-in-contact" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
The first lens bends the beam towards its focus; the second, right behind it, bends it again. Together they act
as one lens whose power is the sum of the two. Slide the second lens's focal length negative to make it concave:
its power is negative, it undoes part of the first, and the focus moves away. Make the two equal and opposite and
the beam goes on parallel, as through a window.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\frac1f = \frac1{f_1} + \frac1{f_2}, \qquad P = P_1 + P_2 .
$$

A 20 cm lens with a 30 cm lens: $P = 5 + 3.33 = 8.33$ D, $f = 12$ cm.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Two ideal lenses a thousandth of a millimetre apart, each met in turn. The traced focus matches the formula to a
part in ten thousand.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Refraction through a prism

<Scenario id="prism" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
A prism bends a ray towards its base at the first face and again at the second. Drag the incoming ray and watch
the deviation. It is large for a glancing ray, falls as the ray steepens, reaches a least value, and rises again.
At the least value the path is symmetrical: the ray inside runs parallel to the base and comes out at the angle
it went in. Measuring that one angle is how the refractive index of a glass is found.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\delta = i + e - A, \qquad r_1 + r_2 = A .
$$

At minimum deviation $i = e$ and $r_1 = r_2 = A/2$, so

$$
n = \frac{\sin\frac{A + D_m}{2}}{\sin\frac A2} .
$$

For $A = 60°$ and $n = 1.5$, $D_m = 37.2°$ at $i = 48.6°$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Two exact refractions. The deviation traced at the predicted angle equals $D_m$ to round-off and is larger six
degrees either side. If the ray inside meets the second face beyond the critical angle it is totally reflected
and leaves by the base; the answers then say it does not come out of the second face.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Dispersion: a prism splits white light

<Scenario id="dispersion" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
White light is every colour together. Send it into a prism and it comes out as a fan: red turned least, violet
most, and the rest in order between.

The reason is that glass slows violet light a little more than red, so its refractive index is slightly larger
for violet. Snell's law then bends violet more at the first face. A glass slab would undo this at its second
face, which is parallel to the first, and the colours would leave side by side going the same way. In a prism
the second face turns the light the same way again, and the small difference grows.

Open the parameters and raise the difference between the two indices. The fan widens. This is the difference
between crown glass, which spreads colours a little, and flint glass, which spreads them twice as much.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Each colour obeys the prism formulas with its own index:

$$
\sin i = n_\lambda \sin r_1, \qquad r_1 + r_2 = A, \qquad n_\lambda \sin r_2 = \sin e, \qquad
\delta_\lambda = i + e - A .
$$

For a thin prism, $\delta = (n - 1)A$, so the angle between violet and red is $(n_v - n_r)A$.

The index is modelled by Cauchy's formula, $n = a + b/\lambda^2$, which fits ordinary glasses well across the
visible.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
White light is seven rays, from 700 nm to 410 nm. Each is traced through the prism with the index the glass has
for that wavelength, and its deviation is read from the direction it leaves in.

Tested: with an index of 1.62 for yellow and 0.04 between violet and red, each colour's traced deviation agrees
with the formula above to one part in a million at 45°, 55° and 70°. Violet is turned through nearly 4° more
than red.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The rainbow

<Scenario id="rainbow" optics>
<div slot="intuition">
A rainbow is sunlight sent back by raindrops. Follow the bright ray into the drop. It bends as it enters, crosses
the drop, and meets the back. Most of it leaves there, but some is reflected. That part crosses again and bends
once more as it comes out, heading back roughly the way it came.

Each colour is bent by its own amount going in and coming out, so red and violet leave in slightly different
directions.

That explains the colours but not the bow. For that, look at the faint rays. Sunlight strikes the drop at every
height, and most of it comes back spread over a wide range of angles, too thin to notice. Slide the height. Near
0.86 of the radius the outgoing direction stops changing: rays a little higher and a little lower all come out
together. Light is concentrated there, at 42° from the point opposite the Sun for red and 41° for violet. Every
drop at that angle from your eye sends you that colour, and the drops at that angle form a circle.

Set the reflections to two for the second, fainter bow. It is higher, near 51°, and its colours are the other
way round.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
A ray striking at angle of incidence $i$, refracted at $r$ and reflected $k$ times inside, is turned through

$$
D = 2(i - r) + k(180^\circ - 2r), \qquad \sin i = n \sin r .
$$

The bow is where $D$ is least, $dD/di = 0$:

$$
\cos^2 i = \frac{n^2 - 1}{k(k + 2)} .
$$

For water, $n = 1.330$ for red and $1.343$ for violet. With $k = 1$ the rays return at $42.5^\circ$ and
$40.7^\circ$ to the incoming light. With $k = 2$ they return at $50.2^\circ$ and $53.4^\circ$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The drop is a circle of water a millimetre in radius. Each ray is refracted in by Snell's law, reflected at the
back the stated number of times, and refracted out. The angle it is turned through agrees with the formula to
one part in a million at five heights, for one reflection and for two.

Two things are left out. The reflection inside is partial: most of the light leaves at the back, and the engine
follows only the part that stays in. And it does not say how bright each direction is; the crowding of the faint
rays is what shows it.
</div>
</Scenario>
