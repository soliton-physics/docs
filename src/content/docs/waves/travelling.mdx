## A travelling wave

<Wave id="travelling-wave">
<div slot="intuition">
Flick one end of a long string and a shape runs along it. Nothing travels with the shape. Watch any one bead: it goes
up and down in the same place, and the bead beside it does the same a moment later. A wave is that lateness, handed
along.

Two different speeds are on screen. The pattern moves along at the wave speed. Each bead moves up and down at a speed
of its own, fastest as it passes through the middle and zero at the top and bottom.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
A wave moving towards $+x$:

$$
y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t + \varphi), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T} .
$$

The pattern moves one wavelength in one period, so

$$
v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k} .
$$

The velocity of a bead is $\partial y/\partial t = -A\omega\cos(kx - \omega t + \varphi)$, at most $A\omega$. Two
points $\Delta x$ apart differ in phase by $\Delta\varphi = 2\pi\,\Delta x/\lambda$.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Open it in the playground and click a crest. Its velocity reads zero and its acceleration is at its largest. Drag
  the point to where the string crosses the axis: now the velocity is largest and the acceleration zero.
- Click a second point exactly one wavelength from the first. The phase difference reads 0°, in step. Move it to half a
  wavelength: 180°, exactly out of step.
- Double the frequency. The wave moves twice as fast here, because the wavelength slider has not moved. Compare with
  the string below, where the speed is fixed.
</div>
</Wave>

## A sound wave

<Wave id="sound-wave">
<div slot="intuition">
Sound is a wave too, but the air moves back and forth along the direction of travel, not across it. The strip under
the graph shows layers of air. Where they crowd together is a compression, marked C; where they thin out, a
rarefaction, R. Those patches travel. Each layer only shuffles to and fro about its own place.

The graph above is the same wave drawn the usual way: how far each layer is from where it belongs. Notice where the
compressions sit. They are not at the crests of that graph but where it crosses zero going down, because that is where
the layers behind have moved forward and the ones ahead have moved back.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With $s$ the displacement of a layer along $x$:

$$
s(x,t) = s_0\sin(kx - \omega t), \qquad \Delta p = -B\,\frac{\partial s}{\partial x} = -B k s_0\cos(kx - \omega t),
$$

where $B$ is the bulk modulus. Pressure and displacement are a quarter of a wavelength out of step. In a gas

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}},
$$

Newton's formula with Laplace's correction, about 340 m/s in air at room temperature.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Click a compression and read its displacement: zero. Click a crest of the graph: the layers there are evenly spaced.
- Raise the frequency. The speed of sound does not change, so the compressions get closer together.
- The displacement is a few thousandths of a millimetre. The strip exaggerates it so that it can be seen.
</div>
</Wave>

## Speed of a wave on a string

<Wave id="wave-on-string">
<div slot="intuition">
How fast a wave runs along a string depends only on the string: how hard it is pulled and how heavy it is. A tighter
string snaps each piece back faster, so the wave goes faster. A heavier string is more sluggish, so it goes slower.

The frequency is up to whoever is shaking the end. Shake faster and the wave does not hurry; the crests just come
closer together.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
For tension $T$ and mass per unit length $\mu$:

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{v}{f} .
$$

Four times the tension doubles the speed.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Change the frequency and watch the speed in the panel: it does not move. The wavelength does.
- Make the tension four times as large. The speed doubles and so does the wavelength.
- Change the amplitude. Nothing about the speed changes: loud and soft travel together.
</div>
</Wave>
