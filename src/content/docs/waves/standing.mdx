## Standing waves on a string

<Wave id="standing-wave">
<div slot="intuition">
A string fixed at both ends, like a guitar's. A wave running along it reflects at one end, runs back, reflects again,
and the string ends up carrying two equal waves going opposite ways. Added together they do not travel at all. Some
points, the nodes, never move. Halfway between them, the antinodes swing the most. Everything between two nodes rises
and falls together.

Only some patterns fit, because both ends have to be nodes: one loop, two, three. Each has its own frequency, and those
are the notes the string can play.
</div>
<div slot="maths">

$$
y = A\sin(kx - \omega t) + A\sin(kx + \omega t) = 2A\,\sin kx\,\cos\omega t .
$$

Nodes are where $\sin kx = 0$, half a wavelength apart. With nodes at both ends of a length $L$:

$$
L = \frac{n\lambda}{2}, \qquad f_n = \frac{n v}{2L} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

</div>
<div slot="try">
- Click a node: the amplitude there reads zero and it has no phase. Click an antinode: the full swing.
- Click two points in the same loop: in step. Put the second in the next loop: exactly out of step.
- Step the harmonic from 1 upward and watch the frequency go up in equal steps.
- Press Remix. The standing wave opens as its two travelling waves; change the amplitude of one and it starts to
  crawl.
</div>
</Wave>

## Air column closed at one end

<Wave id="closed-pipe">
<div slot="intuition">
Blow across the top of a bottle. The air inside sets into a standing wave. At the closed bottom the air has nowhere to
go, so it stays put: a node. At the open top it moves freely: an antinode.

A node at one end and an antinode at the other is a quarter of a wavelength, or three quarters, or five. So the pipe
sounds its fundamental, then three times that, then five times. The even harmonics are missing.
</div>
<div slot="maths">

$$
L = (2n - 1)\frac{\lambda}{4}, \qquad f = (2n - 1)\,\frac{v}{4L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

Where the air moves least, the pressure changes most: the closed end is a displacement node and a pressure antinode.
</div>
<div slot="try">
- Step the mode from 1 to 3 and read the frequency: 170, 510, 850 Hz for a half-metre pipe.
- Halve the length. Every frequency doubles.
</div>
</Wave>

## Air column open at both ends

<Wave id="open-pipe">
<div slot="intuition">
A flute, or any pipe open at both ends. The air moves freely at each end, so both are antinodes, with a node between.
That is half a wavelength, and then two halves, three halves: every harmonic is there.

The longest wave that fits is half as long as in a closed pipe of the same length, so an open pipe sounds an octave
higher.
</div>
<div slot="maths">

$$
L = \frac{n\lambda}{2}, \qquad f_n = \frac{n v}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

</div>
<div slot="try">
- Compare the fundamental with the closed pipe at the same length: 340 Hz against 170 Hz.
- Count the nodes for each harmonic: one, two, three.
</div>
</Wave>

## Beats

<Wave id="beats">
<div slot="intuition">
Play two notes that are almost but not quite the same. For a moment their waves are in step and the sound is loud. A
little later one has gained half a cycle on the other, they cancel, and it is quiet. Then loud again. The swelling is
called beats, and it gets slower as the notes get closer. A musician tunes a string by listening for the beats to
stop.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
At the listener:

$$
s = s_0\cos\omega_1 t + s_0\cos\omega_2 t
  = \Big[2s_0\cos\frac{(\omega_1 - \omega_2)\,t}{2}\Big]\cos\frac{(\omega_1 + \omega_2)\,t}{2} .
$$

A note at the average frequency, with a loudness that rises and falls. It is loud twice in each cycle of the bracket,
so

$$
f_\text{beat} = |f_1 - f_2| .
$$

</div>
<div slot="try">
- Click any point. The little graph in the panel is what a listener standing there hears: the note swelling and
  fading.
- Bring the two frequencies together. The swells stretch out, and at equal frequencies they stop.
- The frequencies here are low, 20 and 22 Hz, so that the separate waves can be seen. At 256 and 260 Hz the same thing
  happens, four times a second.
</div>
</Wave>
