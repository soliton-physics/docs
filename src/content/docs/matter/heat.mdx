Temperature says how hot something is. Heat is energy on the move from something hotter to something cooler.
This chapter is about what heat does when it arrives: it makes things expand, warms them, melts and boils them,
and then moves on.

## Thermal expansion

<Scenario id="thermal-expansion" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Heat a rod and it grows. The growth is tiny, about a hundredth of a millimetre for each metre and each degree,
but it is relentless. A ten-metre steel rail warmed by fifty degrees is six millimetres longer, which is why
rails are laid with gaps and bridges sit on rollers.

If the rod is not allowed to grow, it pushes. The last reading gives the stress in a steel rod held rigidly at
both ends: over a hundred megapascals, enough to buckle a track.

A plate grows in both its directions, so its area gains twice the fraction. A block gains three times in volume.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\Delta L = \alpha L\,\Delta T, \qquad \Delta A = 2\alpha A\,\Delta T, \qquad \Delta V = 3\alpha V\,\Delta T .
$$

Coefficient of linear expansion, in $10^{-6}$ per kelvin: aluminium 25, brass 18, copper 17, iron and steel 12,
glass 9, Pyrex 3.2.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The formula evaluated. The growth in the picture is magnified two hundred times.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Calorimetry: a hot block in cool water

<Scenario id="calorimetry" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Drop a hot block of aluminium into cool water, in a vessel that lets no heat out. Press play. The block cools,
the water warms, and they meet.

They do not meet in the middle. The water warms by ten degrees while the block cools by seventy. Water has an
unusually large specific heat: it takes a great deal of heat to change its temperature. What is equal on the two
sides is not the change in temperature but the heat, as the two readings show.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
m_1 c_1 (T_1 - T) = m_2 c_2 (T - T_2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T = \frac{m_1 c_1 T_1 + m_2 c_2 T_2}{m_1 c_1 + m_2 c_2} .
$$

Specific heat, in J/(kg K): water 4186, ice 2100, aluminium 900, copper 385, lead 128.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Heat is passed from the hotter to the cooler at a rate in proportion to the difference between them, until the
difference is under a hundredth of a degree. For 200 g of aluminium at 100 °C in 300 g of water at 20 °C they
meet at 30.0 °C. If the answer would be above 100 °C the water would boil, and the page says so.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Change of state: heating ice to steam

<Scenario id="heating-curve" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
A heater of steady power under a beaker of ice. Press play and watch the graph.

The temperature rises to 0 °C and stops. The heater is still on, but the heat is going into breaking the ice
apart into water, not into making anything hotter. Only when all the ice has gone does the temperature rise
again. At 100 °C it stops once more, for much longer, while the water turns to steam.

Compare the two flat parts. Boiling the water away takes nearly seven times the heat of melting the ice, and
more than five times the heat of warming the water all the way from 0 to 100 °C. That is why a burn from steam
is so much worse than one from boiling water.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
Q = mc\,\Delta T \ \text{(warming)}, \qquad Q = mL \ \text{(changing state)} .
$$

For water: latent heat of fusion $3.34 \times 10^5$ J/kg, of vaporisation $2.26 \times 10^6$ J/kg.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Heat is added at the heater's power and the temperature and the shares of ice, water and steam are found from
the total. For 100 g of ice at −20 °C and a 500 W heater: 8.4 s to reach 0 °C, 66.8 s to melt, 83.7 s to reach
100 °C and 452 s to boil away. No heat is lost and the steam is kept, at atmospheric pressure.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Conduction along a rod

<Scenario id="heat-conduction" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
A rod has one end in boiling water and the other in ice. Press play. Heat moves in from the hot end, slice by
slice, and after a while the temperature at every point stops changing. Heat is still flowing, steadily, but as
much enters each slice as leaves it.

This rod is two metals joined: copper, a good conductor, then steel, a poor one. The same heat has to get
through both. To push it through the steel takes a steep fall in temperature; through the copper, a gentle one.
So the join sits at 88 °C, close to the hot end.

Swap the two conductivities and the join drops to 12 °C. It is exactly like two resistors in series sharing a
voltage.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
H = \frac{KA\,(T_1 - T_2)}{L} .
$$

For two lengths in series the same $H$ crosses both:

$$
H = \frac{A\,(T_1 - T_2)}{L_1/K_1 + L_2/K_2}, \qquad T_\text{join} = \frac{K_1 T_1 + K_2 T_2}{K_1 + K_2} \ \text{for equal lengths} .
$$

Thermal conductivity, in W/(m K): silver 406, copper 385, aluminium 205, steel 50, glass 0.8, air 0.024.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The rod is cut into forty slices and heat is passed between neighbours by Fourier's law in short steps of time.
Once it has settled, the temperature at the join agrees with the formula to within a degree and the heat
flowing to within 2 %, for the metals either way round and for a rod of one metal.

The rod is lagged, so no heat leaves through its sides. Its settling is shown speeded up: the real thing takes
hours.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Newton's law of cooling

<Scenario id="newtons-cooling" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
A cup of tea cools fast while it is very hot and slowly once it is only warm. The rate at which it loses heat is
in proportion to how much hotter it is than the room.

So the gap between the cup and the room shrinks by the same fraction in each equal interval. If it halves in
seven minutes, it halves again in the next seven. It gets ever closer to the room's temperature and never quite
arrives.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\frac{dT}{dt} = -k\,(T - T_s) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)\,e^{-kt} .
$$

The excess over the room halves in a time $\ln 2 / k$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The law is integrated step by step, and the moment at which the excess has halved is caught and compared with
$\ln 2/k$: they agree to a thousandth. The law itself is an approximation, good when the difference in
temperature is small.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Radiation: Wien's and Stefan's laws

<Scenario id="blackbody-radiation" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Everything gives out radiation because of its temperature. At room temperature it is all infrared and invisible.
Heat a piece of iron and it glows dull red, then orange, then white.

The curve shows how much power is sent out at each wavelength. Raise the temperature and two things happen. The
peak moves to shorter wavelengths: that is Wien's law, and it is how the temperature of a star is read from its
colour. And the whole curve rises steeply: the total power goes as the fourth power of the temperature, which is
Stefan's law. The Sun's surface, at 5800 K, peaks at 500 nm, in the green of the visible.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\lambda_\text{max}\,T = 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}, \qquad
\frac{P}{A} = \sigma T^4, \quad \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4} .
$$

A real surface gives out a fraction $e$ of this, its emissivity.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The curve is Planck's law. The engine finds its peak by search and its area by adding it up over every
wavelength, and compares them with Wien's and Stefan's laws, which are consequences of Planck's. They agree to
two parts in a thousand from 500 K to 10 000 K.
</div>
</Scenario>
