A solid resists being stretched, twisted and squeezed. How much it resists is measured by a modulus: the stress
put on it divided by the strain that results. Stress is force on each unit of area. Strain is the change in
size as a fraction of the size, and has no units.

## Stretching a wire

<Scenario id="stress-strain" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Hang a weight on a wire and it gets longer. Double the weight and it gets longer by twice as much. Take the
weight off and it goes back. That is Hooke's law, and a material obeying it is called elastic.

Open the parameters and raise the load. The dot climbs the straight part of the graph. Keep going and it reaches
the elastic limit. Past that, the wire stretches far more for each extra kilogram, and it no longer returns to
its old length when unloaded: it has been permanently deformed. A little further and it breaks.

Try a thicker wire. The same load gives less stress, because it is shared over more area. That is why stress,
not force, is the thing to compare.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\text{stress} = \frac{F}{A}, \qquad \text{strain} = \frac{\Delta L}{L}, \qquad
Y = \frac{\text{stress}}{\text{strain}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta L = \frac{FL}{AY} .
$$

The energy stored in the stretched wire is $\tfrac12 F\,\Delta L$.

Young's modulus, in GPa: steel 200, copper 110, brass 100, aluminium 70.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
In the elastic region this is Hooke's law evaluated. A 10 kg load on a steel wire 1 mm across and 2 m long gives
a stress of 125 MPa and an extension of 1.25 mm.

Beyond the elastic limit the wire is taken to yield forty times more easily and to break at 1.6 times the limit.
Those are round figures that give the curve the shape a ductile metal has. The stretch in the picture is
magnified sixty times.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Shearing a block

<Scenario id="shear-modulus" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Hold the bottom of a block still and push its top face sideways. The block does not get longer or shorter. It
leans, like a pack of cards pushed from the top. This is shear.

The stress is the force divided by the area of the face it acts along, not the face it pushes against. The
strain is how far the top has moved, divided by the height of the block.

Solids resist shear about a third as strongly as they resist stretching. Liquids do not resist it at all, which
is the difference between a solid and a liquid.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
G = \frac{F/A}{\Delta x/h} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta x = \frac{Fh}{AG} .
$$

Shear modulus, in GPa: steel 84, copper 42, aluminium 25, lead 5.6.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The default is the textbook's example: a force of 90 kN along a face of 0.05 m² on a lead block half a metre
tall moves the top by 0.16 mm. The lean in the picture is magnified five hundred times.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Squeezing from all sides

<Scenario id="bulk-modulus" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Take a block down into the sea. The water presses on it equally from every side, and it gets a little smaller
without changing shape. The bulk modulus says how much pressure it takes.

Water itself can be squeezed, though not much: three kilometres down, where the pressure is three hundred
atmospheres, a litre of water has lost fourteen millilitres. Set the modulus to that of steel, 160 GPa, and the
change is seventy times smaller again. Gases are at the other extreme, which is why they are called
compressible and liquids are not.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
B = -\frac{p}{\Delta V/V} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta V}{V} = -\frac{p}{B}, \qquad p = \rho g h .
$$

Bulk modulus, in GPa: steel 160, copper 140, glass 37, water 2.2.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The pressure is that of sea water of density 1030 kg/m³. At 3000 m it is 30 MPa, and water of bulk modulus
2.2 GPa loses 1.4 % of its volume. The shrinking in the picture is magnified ten times.
</div>
</Scenario>
