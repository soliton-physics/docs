A fluid is anything that flows: a liquid or a gas. It cannot push sideways on a surface the way a solid can, so
at rest it pushes only at right angles, and that push on each unit of area is its pressure.

## Pressure and depth

<Scenario id="pressure-depth" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
The water at any depth is holding up all the water above it. So the pressure grows as you go down, by the same
amount for every metre. The arrows on the gauge show the other thing about pressure: at any one point it is the
same in every direction.

Ten metres of water adds about one atmosphere. Change the liquid to mercury, at 13 600 kg/m³, and three quarters
of a metre is enough, which is how a barometer works.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
P = P_0 + \rho g h .
$$

$P_0$ is the pressure on the surface, here one atmosphere, $1.013 \times 10^5$ Pa. The part $\rho g h$ is the
gauge pressure.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The formula evaluated, with $g = 9.8$ m/s². At 10 m in water the gauge pressure is 98 kPa and the total 199 kPa.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Pascal's law and the hydraulic lift

<Scenario id="hydraulic-lift" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Push on a liquid that has nowhere to go and the extra pressure is felt everywhere in it at once. That is
Pascal's law.

Join a narrow cylinder to a wide one. A small force on the narrow piston makes a pressure, and that same
pressure acts on every square centimetre of the wide piston. There are many more of them, so the force there is
much larger. Press play and watch what it costs: the small piston goes down a long way and the large one rises
only a little. The work put in equals the work got out.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_2 = F_1\frac{A_2}{A_1}, \qquad
A_1 d_1 = A_2 d_2 .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The liquid is taken as incompressible, so the volume leaving one cylinder enters the other. With pistons 2 cm
and 20 cm across, 100 N becomes 10 000 N, and a push of 20 cm raises the load by 2 mm.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Archimedes' principle: floating and sinking

<Scenario id="buoyancy" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Press play. The block drops into the liquid, bobs, and settles. What decides where?

The liquid pushes up on the block with a force equal to the weight of the liquid the block has pushed out of the
way. As the block sinks in, that force grows. A block less dense than the liquid reaches a depth where the
upthrust equals its weight, and stays there with part of it above the surface. The share under the surface is
simply the ratio of the two densities: ice, at 920, floats in sea water, at 1030, with nine tenths of it hidden.

A block denser than the liquid is never held up enough, even fully under, and goes to the bottom. There it
presses down with less than its weight, the difference being the upthrust.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
F_\text{up} = \rho_\text{liquid}\,V_\text{under}\,g, \qquad
\frac{V_\text{under}}{V} = \frac{\rho_\text{block}}{\rho_\text{liquid}} \ \text{when floating} .
$$

Apparent weight when wholly under: $(\rho_\text{block} - \rho_\text{liquid})Vg$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The cube is moved by Newton's second law under its weight, the upthrust on the part of it that is under, and a
drag from the liquid, until it stops. The share under the surface is then measured. Tested for five pairs of
densities, floating and sinking: it agrees with the ratio of densities to a tenth of a percent.

The surface is taken to stay level, as in a wide tank.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Bernoulli's principle in a Venturi tube

<Scenario id="bernoulli" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Water flows steadily through a pipe with a narrow middle. As much water must pass through the narrow part each
second as through the wide part, so there it has to go faster. Press play and follow one speck: it races through
the throat and slows again.

Now look at the two gauges. Each is an open tube in which the water stands as high as the pressure below will
hold it. Over the throat it stands lower. Where the water goes faster, its pressure is less.

That is Bernoulli's principle, and it is only the conservation of energy. To speed up, the water must be pushed
from behind harder than it is held back in front. So the pressure behind, in the wide part, must be the greater.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2, \qquad P + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant along a streamline} .
$$

For a level pipe: $P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2)$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The specks are carried along at the speed continuity gives for the bore where they are, and one of them is
timed through the throat. Its speed agrees with $v_1 A_1/A_2$ to within 2 %. The flow is shown slowed so that it
can be followed. The water is taken to have no viscosity and to flow without turbulence.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Torricelli's law: a tank emptying

<Scenario id="torricelli" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Water leaves a hole in the side of a tank at the speed it would have if it had fallen freely from the surface
down to the hole. This is Bernoulli's principle again, applied between the surface and the hole.

Press play. The jet starts strong and lands far out. As the level falls, the jet weakens and its landing point
creeps back toward the tank. The level itself falls fast at first and ever more slowly: the last few centimetres
take as long as the first half.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
v = \sqrt{2gh}, \qquad \text{range} = v\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}} = 2\sqrt{hH} .
$$

With a hole of area $a$ in a tank of area $A$, the level obeys $dh/dt = -(a/A)\sqrt{2gh}$, which gives

$$
h = h_0\left(1 - \frac{t}{T}\right)^2, \qquad T = \frac{A}{a}\sqrt{\frac{2h_0}{g}} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The level is stepped down at the rate the hole carries water away. The time it takes to empty agrees with $T$ to
within 1 % for three tanks. With 1 m of water over a hole 1 m above the ground, the jet starts at 4.43 m/s and
lands 2 m out. The run is shown speeded up; the clock is in real seconds.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Viscosity and terminal velocity

<Scenario id="stokes-law" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
Drop a steel ball into glycerine. It does not keep accelerating as it would in air. Within a few hundredths of a
second it is moving at a steady speed and stays at it all the way down.

Three forces act: its weight down, the upthrust up, and the drag of the liquid, also up. The drag is larger the
faster the ball moves. So the ball speeds up until the drag has grown enough to make the three balance, and
after that there is no net force and no more acceleration.

The graph shows the first few milliseconds, where the speed climbs to its terminal value. A ball twice the size
falls four times as fast.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Stokes's law for the drag on a sphere moving slowly: $F = 6\pi\eta r v$. Setting weight equal to upthrust plus
drag:

$$
v_t = \frac{2r^2(\rho - \sigma)g}{9\eta} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The ball is integrated under the three forces. For a 2 mm steel ball in glycerine its speed settles at
68.6 mm/s, agreeing with the formula to a part in a thousand.

Stokes's law holds only for smooth flow round the ball, at a Reynolds number below about one. The readings give
that number and warn when the law is being stretched.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Surface tension and capillary rise

<Scenario id="capillary-rise" matter>
<div slot="intuition">
The surface of a liquid behaves like a stretched skin. Where water meets glass, which it wets, that skin pulls
the water up the glass. In a fine tube the pull round the rim is enough to hold up a column of water, and the
finer the tube the taller the column, because a thinner column weighs less for each millimetre of rim.

Set the angle of contact past 90° and the density to mercury's. Mercury does not wet glass, the surface curves
the other way, and the liquid in the tube is pushed down below the level outside.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
h = \frac{2S\cos\theta}{\rho g r} .
$$

The same surface tension makes the pressure inside a drop greater than outside by $2S/r$, and inside a soap
bubble, which has two surfaces, by $4S/r$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The formula evaluated. Water, with $S = 0.073$ N/m, rises 30 mm in a tube of radius 0.5 mm and half as far in
one twice as wide.
</div>
</Scenario>
