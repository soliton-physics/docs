These pages cover three chapters of NCERT class 11: mechanical properties of solids, mechanical properties of
fluids, and thermal properties of matter. They are in the Matter part of the playground.

Mechanics treated every body as rigid and every fluid as absent. These chapters put back what was left out: a
wire stretches, water presses and pushes up, a flowing liquid trades pressure for speed, and things grow, melt
and cool.

## What is followed and what is a formula

Where something happens in time, the engine steps it forward and the textbook result is what it is compared
with:

- **A floating block** is let go and bobs under its weight, the upthrust and the liquid's drag until it comes to
  rest. The share of it under the surface is then read off.
- **A draining tank** has its level stepped down at the rate the hole carries water away.
- **A falling ball** is integrated under weight, upthrust and Stokes's drag until its speed stops changing.
- **A speck in a pipe** is carried along at the local speed and timed through the throat.
- **Heat along a rod** is passed from slice to slice by Fourier's law until the temperatures stop changing.
- **A hot block in water, heated ice and a cooling cup** are each stepped forward in time.
- **A glowing body's** peak wavelength and total power are found from Planck's curve by search and by summing.

The rest are laws evaluated with a picture: Hooke's law, pressure at a depth, Pascal's law, capillary rise,
thermal expansion.

## Limits

- **Changes in solids are magnified to be seen.** A steel wire stretches by a thousandth of its length. Each
  picture says by how much it exaggerates; the numbers beside it do not.
- **The curve beyond the elastic limit is a sketch.** It has the right shape for a ductile metal, with round
  figures, and is not the measured curve of any one alloy.
- **Fluids are ideal where the textbook's are:** no viscosity in the Venturi tube or the draining tank, no
  turbulence anywhere. Stokes's law is used only where it holds, and the page warns when the Reynolds number
  says it does not.
- **No heat is lost** to the surroundings in calorimetry or along the rod.
- **Not built yet:** the streamlines round a wing, Poiseuille flow in a pipe, convection, and thermal stress in
  a structure.
