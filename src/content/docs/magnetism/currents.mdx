A current makes a magnetic field, and a magnetic field pushes on a current. In the pictures a wire that cuts the
page is a circle: a dot in it means the current comes towards you, a cross means it goes away. The small needles
are compasses, red end along the field, fainter where the field is weaker.

## A straight wire

<Scenario id="straight-wire-field" magnetism>
<div slot="intuition">
The field goes round the wire in circles. Grip the wire with your right hand, thumb along the current, and your
fingers curl the way the field points.

Open the parameters and move the point away. At twice the distance the field is half as strong. Even 5 A at
5 cm gives only half the earth's field, which is why a compass has to be close to a wire to notice it.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The Biot–Savart law gives the field of a short piece of wire $d\boldsymbol{l}$:

$$
d\boldsymbol{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\boldsymbol{l}\times\boldsymbol{r}}{r^3} .
$$

Added up along a long straight wire, or found in one line from Ampere's law,

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} , \qquad \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m/A} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The engine cuts a wire 400 times as long as the distance into 40 000 pieces and adds up the Biot–Savart field of
each. The answer chip shows the formula and, beside it, that sum. They agree to about one part in 100 000; what
is missing is the part of an endless wire beyond the ends.
</div>
</Scenario>

## A circular coil

<Scenario id="circular-loop-field" magnetism>
<div slot="intuition">
The coil is seen edge on. Its current comes out of the page at the top and goes in at the bottom. Each side
makes circles of field round itself, and through the middle the two agree, so the field there is strong and
straight.

Follow the needles round: out through one face, back round the outside, in at the other. That is the pattern of
a short bar magnet, and from far away a coil is one, with moment $NIA$.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
On the axis, a distance $x$ from the centre of a coil of $N$ turns and radius $R$:

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 N I R^2}{2\,(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}} , \qquad
B_{\text{centre}} = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2R} , \qquad
B \to \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2m}{x^3}\ \ (x \gg R),\quad m = NIA .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
One turn is 720 straight pieces. The field on the axis is their sum, compared with the formula. The needles off
the axis come from the same sum with 48 pieces, since no simple formula exists there.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The solenoid

<Scenario id="solenoid-field" magnetism>
<div slot="intuition">
Wind many turns side by side. Inside, every turn adds to the same straight field. Outside, the near and far
sides of the winding nearly cancel. The result is an even field inside and almost none outside.

Slide the point to the end of the solenoid (100 %). The field there is half of what it is deep inside, because
only the turns on one side are left. Make the solenoid longer and thinner and the middle gets closer to the
ideal $\mu_0 n I$.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With $n$ turns in each metre:

$$
B = \mu_0 n I \quad\text{(deep inside a long solenoid)} , \qquad
B(x) = \tfrac12\mu_0 n I\,(\cos\theta_1 + \cos\theta_2) ,
$$

where $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are the angles at which the two rims are seen from the point on the axis. At an
end one of them is 90° and the other nearly 0°, which gives half.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The field of every turn is added, using the formula for a coil's axis. That sum is compared with the formula for
a solenoid of this length. The picture stands 24 loops for the winding and sums their Biot–Savart fields for the
needles.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Two parallel wires

<Scenario id="parallel-currents" magnetism>
<div slot="intuition">
Each wire sits in the other's field and is pushed by it. Currents in the same direction attract. Make the second
current negative and they repel.

Look at the needles between the wires. With like currents the two fields oppose there and nearly cancel; with
unlike currents they add. Field lines act a little like stretched elastic that also push apart sideways, and
that picture gives the right answer in both cases.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The first wire makes $B = \mu_0 I_1/2\pi d$ at the second, which feels $F = I_2 L B$:

$$
\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d} .
$$

Until 2019 this defined the ampere: the current which, in each of two long wires 1 m apart, gives
$2\times10^{-7}$ N on each metre.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The field of the first wire at the second is the Biot–Savart sum, multiplied by the second current.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Force on a wire in a field

<Scenario id="force-on-a-wire" magnetism>
<div slot="intuition">
The field runs left to right. A wire across it is pushed out of the page or into it, never along the field and
never along itself. Turn the wire towards the field and the push fades; along the field it is gone.

This sideways push on a current is what turns every electric motor and moves the cone of every loudspeaker.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\boldsymbol{F} = I\,\boldsymbol{L}\times\boldsymbol{B} , \qquad F = ILB\sin\theta .
$$

Fleming's left-hand rule gives the direction: first finger along the field, second along the current, thumb
along the force.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
This is the law evaluated. It is the same force as $q\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}$ on each moving charge,
which the [mechanics scenarios](/electromagnetism/magnetism/) follow one charge at a time.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The moving-coil galvanometer

<Scenario id="moving-coil-galvanometer" magnetism>
<div slot="intuition">
A coil hangs between the poles of a magnet, on a fine spring. Press play: a current is let in, the two sides of
the coil are pushed opposite ways, and it turns until the spring holds it. The pointer overshoots once and
settles.

Twice the current gives twice the deflection. That even scale is the reason for the curved pole pieces: they
keep the field in the plane of the coil however far it has turned.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\tau = NIAB , \qquad k\phi = NIAB \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi = \frac{NAB}{k}\,I .
$$

$NAB/k$ is the current sensitivity. A shunt across the coil makes an ammeter of it; a large resistance in series
makes a voltmeter.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The coil is stepped forward under the torque, the spring and some damping. Its period (1.2 s) and damping are
chosen so the settling can be watched; where it settles does not depend on them, and is compared with $NIAB/k$.
</div>
</Scenario>
