## Rutherford's alpha scattering

<Scenario id="rutherford-scattering" modern height={480}>
<div slot="intuition">
In 1911 the atom was thought to be a ball of positive charge with electrons dotted through it. Geiger and
Marsden fired alpha particles at a thin gold foil to test that. Nearly all went straight through. About one in
eight thousand came back.

Press play. Each line is an alpha particle, coming in from the left past one gold nucleus. Those that pass far
from it are barely turned. The closer ones swing through large angles, and the one aimed straight at it stops
and returns the way it came. The dashed circle is as near as that one gets.

For that to happen, all the positive charge and nearly all the mass must be in something far smaller than the
atom. The stage here is 840 femtometres across. On this scale the whole atom would be more than a hundred of these
stages wide.

Change the impact parameter of the amber alpha, how far off-centre it is aimed, and watch its angle.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
A head-on alpha stops where its kinetic energy has all become potential energy:

$$
d = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2Ze^2}{E} .
$$

One aimed to miss by $b$, the impact parameter, is turned through

$$
\theta = 2\arctan\frac{d}{2b} .
$$

A handy number: $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1.44$ MeV fm. For a 7.7 MeV alpha and gold, $Z = 79$:
$d = 2 \times 79 \times 1.44 / 7.7 = 30$ fm.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Each alpha is integrated with Newton's second law under Coulomb's force from a fixed nucleus, from 40 000 fm
away to 40 000 fm away, with shorter steps where the path bends. The formula for the angle is not used.

Tested: the head-on alpha at 7.7 MeV turns back at 29.5 fm, the textbook's value. At impact parameters of 10,
30, 80 and 200 fm the angle agrees with Rutherford's formula to within 0.15 degrees.

The nucleus is held still. A real gold nucleus recoils a little, which changes the angle by about 2 %.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Bohr's model of the hydrogen atom

<Scenario id="bohr-model" modern height={480}>
<div slot="intuition">
Rutherford's atom had a problem. An electron circling a nucleus is accelerating, so it should radiate, lose
energy, and spiral into the nucleus in far less than a billionth of a second. Atoms do not collapse.

Bohr's way out, in 1913, was to say that only certain orbits are allowed, and in those the electron does not
radiate. Each has a definite energy. Light is given out only when the electron drops from one allowed orbit to a
lower one, and the energy lost leaves as a single photon.

Press play. The electron circles in the upper orbit, drops, and a photon leaves in the colour its energy gives
it. The ladder on the right is the same story in energy. Choose other levels: a fall to $n = 2$ gives visible
light, a fall to $n = 1$ ultraviolet, and to $n = 3$ infrared.

The orbits are drawn to scale, their radii as 1, 4, 9, 16. Set the charge of the nucleus to 2 for a helium atom
that has lost one electron: every energy is four times as large.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The allowed orbits have angular momentum $n\,h/2\pi$. With Coulomb's force as the centripetal force:

$$
r_n = 0.0529\,\frac{n^2}{Z}\ \text{nm}, \qquad E_n = -13.6\,\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\ \text{eV}, \qquad
v_n = \frac{c}{137}\,\frac{Z}{n} .
$$

$$
hf = E_{n_2} - E_{n_1}, \qquad \frac{1}{\lambda} = R\,Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right), \qquad
R = 1.097 \times 10^7\ \text{m}^{-1} .
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The energies, radii and wavelengths are Bohr's formulas with the measured constants. The red line from 3 to 2
comes out at 656.1 nm and the first Lyman line at 121.5 nm. The electron is drawn turning with a period that
goes as $n^3$, as it does in the model, slowed by a factor of some $10^{15}$ so that it can be seen.

The model is right about these energies and wrong about the picture. It fails for atoms with more than one
electron, and quantum mechanics replaced the orbits with clouds of probability. It is in the syllabus because it
was the first step, and because its energies are exactly right for hydrogen.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The line spectrum of hydrogen

<Scenario id="hydrogen-spectrum" modern height={420}>
<div slot="intuition">
Pass a current through hydrogen and look at its glow through a prism. There is no rainbow, only a few sharp
lines: red, blue-green, and violets crowding together. Balmer found a formula for their wavelengths in 1885
without knowing why it worked. Bohr's model is the why.

Each series is all the falls that end on one level. The Balmer series ends on $n = 2$ and is the only one the
eye can see. Lyman ends on $n = 1$ and is in the ultraviolet. Paschen, Brackett and Pfund end on 3, 4 and 5, in
the infrared.

Step through the lines of a series. They get closer and closer and stop at a limit, the wavelength for an
electron falling in from far away. The scale along the bottom is one of ratios, so that all five series fit.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right), \qquad n_2 = n_1 + 1,\ n_1 + 2,\ \dots
$$

The longest wavelength of a series has $n_2 = n_1 + 1$. The series limit has $n_2 \to \infty$:
$\lambda = n_1^2/R$, which is 91.1 nm for Lyman and 364.5 nm for Balmer.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Every line is computed from the energy levels. The Balmer lines come out at 656.1, 486.0, 433.9 and 410.1 nm,
against measured values in air of 656.3, 486.1, 434.0 and 410.2: the small difference is the motion of the
proton and the air, which the simple formula leaves out.
</div>
</Scenario>
