## Radioactive decay

<Scenario id="radioactive-decay" modern height={480}>
<div slot="intuition">
Each dot is an unstable nucleus. Press play and they begin to decay, here and there, with no pattern. You
cannot tell which will go next, and neither can anyone else: a nucleus that has lasted a long time is no more
likely to decay in the next second than one made a moment ago.

Yet look at the graph. The number left follows the dashed curve closely, halving in each half-life: 900, then
450, then 225. The certainty comes from the numbers. One nucleus is a coin toss. A million million of them are a
law.

Set the sample to 100 nuclei and run it a few times. Now the curve is ragged, and the count after one half-life
might be 43 or 56. Set it to 2500 and it is smooth. Real samples hold far more, which is why half-lives can be
measured to many figures.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
If each nucleus has a chance $\lambda\,dt$ of decaying in a short time $dt$:

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N, \qquad N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad
T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{T_{1/2}}{0.693} .
$$

The activity is $R = \lambda N$, in becquerel: decays a second.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
There is no exponential in the program's rule. In each short step every surviving nucleus is given the same
small chance of decaying, with a seeded random number generator so that a run can be repeated.

Tested with 2500 nuclei: the number left after one and two half-lives is within three standard deviations of
$N_0/2$ and $N_0/4$, and the mean age at decay is within 0.4 s of $T/\ln 2$ for a half-life of 4 s.

The answers on the stage show what this sample did beside what the law says, with a tick when they agree to
within the scatter chance allows.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The binding energy curve

<Scenario id="binding-energy" modern height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
A nucleus weighs less than the protons and neutrons it is made of. The missing mass is the energy that was given
out when it formed, and that must be put back to pull it apart: its binding energy. Divide by the number of
nucleons and you have a measure of how tightly each is held.

The curve rises steeply for light nuclei, with helium-4 standing well above its neighbours. It peaks near
iron and nickel at about 8.8 MeV, and then slopes gently down to uranium at 7.6.

Everything about nuclear energy is in that shape. Move the marker to a heavy nucleus: split it in two and both
halves are higher up the curve, so energy comes out. That is fission, and for uranium it is about 200 MeV a
nucleus. Move it to a light one: join two and the result is higher up, so energy comes out again. That is
fusion, which powers the Sun. Near iron neither pays, which is why iron is where stars stop.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\Delta m = Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n - M, \qquad E_b = \Delta m\,c^2, \qquad 1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}/c^2 .
$$

The liquid-drop formula treats the nucleus as a drop of charged liquid:

$$
E_b = a_V A - a_S A^{2/3} - a_C \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}} - a_A \frac{(A - 2Z)^2}{A} \pm \delta ,
$$

a term for every nucleon's neighbours, less for those at the surface, less for the protons' repulsion, less for
an imbalance of protons and neutrons, and a little more when they are paired.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The dots are measured values from the atomic mass tables for thirty nuclei. The line is the liquid-drop formula
with $a_V = 15.75$, $a_S = 17.8$, $a_C = 0.711$, $a_A = 23.7$ and $\delta = 11.18/\sqrt A$ MeV, at the number of
protons that binds each mass number most tightly.

Tested: the formula gives 26 protons as the most stable for 56 nucleons, and is within 0.12 MeV per nucleon of
every measured value from calcium upward. From the measured values, deuterium and tritium fusing to helium-4
and a neutron release 17.59 MeV. The readings for splitting and fusing use the formula, so they are rough
estimates, and unreliable below about mass number 20.
</div>
</Scenario>
