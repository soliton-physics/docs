Light behaves as a wave when it interferes and as a stream of particles when it hits a metal. An electron is a
particle when it is counted and a wave when it meets a crystal. This chapter is the evidence for both.

## The photoelectric effect

<Scenario id="photoelectric-effect" modern height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
Light falls on a metal plate in a vacuum tube. Electrons come out of the plate and cross to a collector, and a
meter counts them. Press play, then open the parameters and try three things.

**Make the light brighter.** More electrons arrive each second. They are no faster.

**Make the wavelength shorter.** The electrons leave with more energy. Their number does not change.

**Make the wavelength longer than about 580 nm.** The electrons stop altogether, and no amount of brightness
brings them back.

A wave cannot do this. A brighter wave should shake electrons harder, and a dim one should free them given time.
Einstein's answer was that light arrives in packets, photons, each with energy $hf$. One photon frees one
electron. Brighter light is more photons, so more electrons. Bluer light is bigger photons, so faster electrons.
And if one photon has less energy than it takes to get an electron out of the metal, nothing happens, however
many photons there are.

Now make the collector negative. It pushes electrons back, and fewer arrive. At one voltage, the stopping
potential, even the fastest are turned round. That voltage measures their greatest energy.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
K_\text{max} = hf - \varphi = \frac{hc}{\lambda} - \varphi, \qquad eV_0 = K_\text{max}, \qquad
\lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{\varphi} .
$$

A handy number: $hc = 1240\ \text{eV nm}$. For caesium, $\varphi = 2.14$ eV, so the threshold is 579 nm. Light of
400 nm has photons of 3.10 eV and frees electrons of up to 0.96 eV, stopped by 0.96 V.

Work functions, in eV: caesium 2.14, potassium 2.30, sodium 2.75, calcium 3.20, aluminium 4.28, copper 4.65,
silver 4.70, platinum 5.65.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Photons arrive at random moments, at a rate set by the brightness. Each that lands frees one electron if
$hf > \varphi$, with an energy drawn evenly between zero and $K_\text{max}$. The electron is then moved across
the tube under the collector's voltage, and counted if it arrives.

Tested: with full brightness the count is 40 a second at any positive voltage, half that at half the stopping
potential, and none beyond it, each within the scatter of a random count. Below the threshold the count is
exactly zero.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The wavelength of an electron

<Scenario id="de-broglie-wavelength" modern height={420}>
<div slot="intuition">
If light, a wave, can act as particles, de Broglie asked in 1924, can particles act as waves? He proposed that
anything with momentum $p$ has a wavelength $h/p$.

The picture draws that wavelength to scale above a row of atoms in a crystal. For an electron sped up through
100 volts it is 0.12 nm, about the gap between the atoms. That is the condition for diffraction, and it is why
a crystal can do to electrons what a grating does to light.

Raise the voltage and the wave shortens: a faster electron has more momentum. The readings also give the
wavelength of a cricket ball. It is some $10^{-34}$ m, smaller than a nucleus by twenty powers of ten, which is
why nobody has seen a ball diffract.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}} = \frac{1.227}{\sqrt{V}}\ \text{nm}
$$

for an electron accelerated from rest through $V$ volts, while it is much slower than light.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
This lesson is the formula, evaluated with the measured constants. At 100 V it gives 0.1226 nm, and the
textbook's shortcut $1.227/\sqrt V$ agrees to four figures. At 1000 V the electron moves at 6 % of the speed of
light and relativity would shorten the wavelength by about 0.05 %; the engine does not include that.
</div>
</Scenario>

## The Davisson–Germer experiment

<Scenario id="davisson-germer" modern height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
In 1927 Davisson and Germer fired electrons at a crystal of nickel and counted how many bounced off at each
angle. If electrons were only particles, the count would fall off smoothly. It did not. At 54 volts there was a
clear hump at 50 degrees.

The rows of atoms on the face of the crystal are 0.215 nm apart. Waves scattered from neighbouring rows arrive
in step in the direction where their paths differ by one wavelength. Put de Broglie's 0.167 nm into that
condition and the angle comes out at 51 degrees. The electrons were diffracting.

Change the voltage. The hump moves: faster electrons have shorter waves and are thrown through smaller angles.
Lower it far enough and the wavelength is longer than the spacing, and there is no hump at all.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
d\sin\theta = n\lambda, \qquad \lambda = \frac{1.227}{\sqrt V}\ \text{nm} .
$$

With $d = 0.215$ nm, $V = 54$ V and $n = 1$: $\sin\theta = 0.167/0.215$, so $\theta = 50.9^\circ$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The angle is fixed by the two lengths. The shape drawn round it is that of a grating of six rows on a background
that fades towards grazing angles, which is a model and not a calculation of nickel. The engine reads the
strongest angle off the pattern as a detector would and it agrees with $\sin\theta = \lambda/d$ to within 1.5
degrees.
</div>
</Scenario>
