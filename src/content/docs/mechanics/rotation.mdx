## Rolling down an incline

<Scenario id="rolling">
<div slot="intuition">
A disc rolling down a slope arrives later than a block sliding down a frictionless one, because part of the energy
goes into spinning. A ring arrives later still: more of its mass is far from the axis. And rolling needs friction.
With a slippery slope the disc slides without turning. Lower the friction slider until the slip event appears.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
For a round body with moment of inertia $I = k m R^2$ rolling without slipping,

$$
a = \frac{g \sin\theta}{1 + k},
$$

which needs static friction $\mu_s \ge \frac{k}{1 + k} \tan\theta$. A disc has $k = \tfrac{1}{2}$, a ring $k = 1$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Rolling is not a special mode in the engine. The contact point's friction is decided like any other, and when it holds,
the point does not slide, so $v = \omega R$ falls out. When $\mu_s$ is too small the same rule produces slipping and
the textbook sliding acceleration. Validated to 1e-8 in both regimes.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Atwood machine with a massive pulley

<Scenario id="atwood-massive">
<div slot="intuition">
In the ideal Atwood machine the string's tension is the same on both sides. Give the pulley mass and that can no
longer be true: something has to spin the pulley up, and the only thing touching it is the string. So the heavier
side pulls a little harder than the lighter side, and the difference supplies the torque. The whole system
accelerates more slowly than with a massless pulley, because the pulley's inertia is along for the ride.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With a uniform disc of mass $M$, $I = \tfrac{1}{2} M R^2$, and the no-slip condition $a = \alpha R$,

$$
a = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)\, g}{m_1 + m_2 + M/2}, \qquad T_1 = m_1 (g + a), \qquad T_2 = m_2 (g - a).
$$

The torque on the pulley is $(T_2 - T_1) R = I \alpha$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The pulley is a rigid disc pinned at its centre, and the rope wraps it as a wheel: the rope's tangent points are
treated as material points of the rim, so the rope segment on each side is its own constraint and the no-slip
coupling falls out of the geometry. Validated to 1e-8 for the acceleration, both tensions and the rim speed.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Flywheel with a hanging mass

<Scenario id="flywheel">
<div slot="intuition">
Wind a string around a heavy wheel and hang a mass from it. The mass falls, but slowly: most of the energy goes into
spinning the wheel. Double the wheel's mass and watch the fall slow further. This is how a flywheel stores energy.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$$
a = \frac{m g}{m + I / R^2} = \frac{m g}{m + M/2}, \qquad T = m (g - a).
$$
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Same wheel node as the massive pulley, with one end of the rope fixed to the rim. Validated to 1e-8.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Seesaw

<Scenario id="seesaw">
<div slot="intuition">
A seesaw balances when the torques match, not the masses. A child far from the pivot balances a heavier adult close
to it. Move a mass outward with the slider and the plank tips, slowly at first, because the whole system has to be
spun up.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Balance when $m_1 d_1 = m_2 d_2$. Otherwise the initial angular acceleration is

$$
\alpha = \frac{(m_1 d_1 - m_2 d_2)\, g}{I_{plank} + m_1 d_1^2 + m_2 d_2^2},
$$

counter-clockwise positive.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The plank is pinned to the ground and each mass is pinned to the plank: three exact constraints solved together.
Balanced, the plank's angular velocity stays below 1e-9. Tipping, the initial angular acceleration matches the
formula to 1e-9. This scenario is also the one that caught a solver bug: the position projection has to be
Gauss–Seidel, not Jacobi, or three coupled pins diverge.
</div>
</Scenario>
