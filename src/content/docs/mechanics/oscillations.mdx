## Mass on a spring

<Scenario id="spring-mass">
<div slot="intuition">
Pull a block on a spring and let go. It overshoots the middle, slows, comes back, overshoots again: simple harmonic
motion. The surprising fact is that the period does not depend on how far you pulled it. A bigger amplitude means
a bigger speed through the middle, and the two cancel exactly. Watch kinetic and potential energy trade places in the
energy graph while their sum stays flat.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
$m \ddot{x} = -k x$ gives $x = A \cos(\omega t)$ with

$$
\omega = \sqrt{k/m}, \qquad T = 2\pi \sqrt{m/k}.
$$

Maximum speed $A \omega$, maximum force $k A$, energy $\tfrac{1}{2} k A^2$ throughout.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The spring is a force with a potential, so its energy appears in the ledger. Velocity Verlet is symplectic: the energy
oscillates slightly within a period but never drifts, and the measured period matches the known Verlet dispersion
formula to 1e-8 and the textbook period to $(\omega \Delta t)^2 / 12$.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Simple pendulum

<Scenario id="simple-pendulum">
<div slot="intuition">
For small swings the pendulum keeps time regardless of amplitude, which is why clocks used them for three centuries.
Pull it back far, say 120°, and the period grows noticeably: the restoring force is no longer proportional to the
displacement. The playground shows both the small-angle formula and the exact period.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Small angles: $T_0 = 2\pi \sqrt{L/g}$. Any amplitude $\theta_0$:

$$
T = 4 \sqrt{L/g}\, K\!\left(\sin \tfrac{\theta_0}{2}\right),
$$

with $K$ the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, computed by the arithmetic–geometric mean.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The string is a rod constraint solved with RATTLE, which keeps the length exact and the energy drift at 1e-10 over
twenty periods. At 2 rad amplitude the period matches the elliptic integral to 1.7e-8 with second-order convergence.
</div>
</Scenario>
