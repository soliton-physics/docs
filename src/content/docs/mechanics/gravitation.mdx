These scenes are in space around the Earth, drawn to scale: the planet is 6371 km in radius and the distances are
real. An orbit takes about an hour and a half, so the playground plays these a few hundred times faster than life;
the clock still shows the real time.

## Satellite in a circular orbit

<Scenario id="circular-orbit" height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
A satellite is not beyond gravity. At 400 km up, gravity is still nine tenths of what it is on the ground. It stays up
because it moves sideways so fast that, as it falls, the ground curves away beneath it just as quickly. It is falling
the whole time and never getting closer.

Drag the satellite outward and watch the speed it needs drop and its year lengthen. At 35 786 km the period is one
day, which is where satellites that seem to hang still in the sky are parked.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force:

$$
\frac{G M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}, \qquad T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{r^3}{G M}} .
$$

The satellite's mass cancels: a spanner let go outside the station stays beside it.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Newton's law is a force like any other, pointing at the Earth's centre, and the engine's stepping is symplectic, so
the orbit neither spirals in nor drifts out however long it runs. At a few thousand steps a turn the speed stays
constant to 1e-4 and the energy to 1e-9. The scene runs a little over two turns, so the period you see measured is
the time between two passes of the same point.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Elliptical orbit

<Scenario id="elliptical-orbit" height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
Give the satellite more than the circular speed and it climbs away, slowing as it goes, until it has too little speed
to stay that far out and falls back, speeding up, to exactly where it started. The path is an ellipse with the centre
of the Earth at one focus.

Watch the velocity arrow: long at the near point, short at the far one. The line from the Earth to the satellite
sweeps the same area every minute, which is Kepler's second law, and is nothing more than angular momentum being
conserved.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With speed $v_p$ at the nearest point, a distance $r_p$ from the centre, the energy per unit mass and the shape are

$$
\varepsilon = \tfrac12 v_p^2 - \frac{G M}{r_p}, \qquad
a = -\frac{G M}{2 \varepsilon}, \qquad
e = \frac{r_p v_p^2}{G M} - 1 .
$$

The farthest point is at $r_a = a(1 + e)$, where $r_a v_a = r_p v_p$. Kepler's third law gives the period from the
semi-major axis alone: $T = 2\pi \sqrt{a^3 / G M}$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Nothing is told that the path is an ellipse; the engine only adds up the pull of gravity step by step. The farthest
point and the slowest speed come out right to 1e-5. The areal velocity $\tfrac12\, r \times v$ is constant to
round-off, 1e-10, because the stepping scheme conserves angular momentum exactly for a force that points at a
centre.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Escape velocity

<Scenario id="escape-velocity" height={460}>
<div slot="intuition">
Throw something straight up and it comes back. Throw it harder and it goes higher before it does. But gravity
weakens with distance, so the total it can take from the thing's motion on the way out is finite. Leave with more
than that and it never comes back: gravity slows it for ever and never stops it.

For the Earth that speed is 11.2 km/s. Drag the launch arrow across it and see the answer change from a maximum height
to a speed left over.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Energy conservation between the surface, radius $R$, and far away:

$$
\tfrac12 u^2 - \frac{G M}{R} \ge 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{R}} = \sqrt{2 g R} .
$$

Below it, the height reached is $h = R \,/\, (v_\text{esc}^2 / u^2 - 1)$; above it, the speed far away is
$\sqrt{u^2 - v_\text{esc}^2}$. Note $h$ is not $u^2 / 2g$: that formula assumes $g$ stays the same all the way up.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The rocket is a body with one force on it, and the scene ends when it touches the Earth again. Its maximum height
matches the formula to 1e-5, and above the escape speed its speed ten Earth radii out agrees with energy
conservation to 1e-5. A launch is followed for at most six hours: just under the escape speed the return takes days.
</div>
</Scenario>
