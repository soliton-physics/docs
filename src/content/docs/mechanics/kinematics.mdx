## Free fall

<Scenario id="free-fall">
<div slot="intuition">
Drop a ball and it speeds up. Not gradually faster and faster in a vague way: its speed grows by the same 9.8 m/s
every second. Watch the velocity graph in the playground. It is a straight line, and its slope is $g$.
The position graph is a parabola because distance grows with the square of time.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With $u = 0$ and acceleration $g$ downward,

$$
v = g t, \qquad h = \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 .
$$

So a ball dropped from height $h$ lands after $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$ with speed $v = \sqrt{2 g h}$.
Doubling the height does not double the time: it multiplies it by $\sqrt{2}$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The engine integrates Newton's second law with velocity Verlet, which is exact for constant acceleration, so the
landing position is reproduced to round-off at any time step. The ground is a contact with restitution zero, so the
ball stops instead of bouncing. Test: `projectile_is_exact_at_any_step` in `soliton-mechanics`.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Ball thrown upward

<Scenario id="thrown-upward">
<div slot="intuition">
The ball slows down on the way up, stops for an instant at the top, and falls back. The trap is to think the
acceleration is zero at the top. It is not. The velocity is zero there, but it is changing at 9.8 m/s² the whole time,
which is exactly why the ball does not stay up.
</div>
<div slot="maths">

$$
v = u - g t, \qquad y = u t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 .
$$

Maximum height when $v = 0$: $H = u^2 / 2g$ at $t = u/g$. The flight is symmetric, so the total time is $2u/g$ and
the ball returns at speed $u$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Same integrator as free fall. The acceleration readout in the playground stays at 9.8 m/s² through the top, which is
the point of the lesson.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Projectile

<Scenario id="projectile">
<div slot="intuition">
A projectile is two motions happening at once that do not know about each other. Horizontally nothing pushes or
pulls, so the horizontal speed never changes. Vertically it is a ball thrown upward. Put them together and the path
is a parabola. Tilt the launch angle in the playground: 45° gives the longest range, and angles equally above and
below 45° land in the same place.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With launch speed $u$ at angle $\theta$,

$$
x = u \cos\theta \, t, \qquad y = u \sin\theta \, t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 .
$$

Time of flight $T = 2 u \sin\theta / g$, range $R = u^2 \sin 2\theta / g$, maximum height $H = u^2 \sin^2\theta / 2g$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The ground is a half-plane contact. The engine reports the landing as a `contact_begin` event, visible in the event log.
Impact timing is located to within one time step, so the landing time is accurate to a millisecond at the default step.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Projectile from a cliff

<Scenario id="projectile-from-height">
<div slot="intuition">
Launched from a height, the projectile has further to fall, so it stays in the air longer and travels further.
Try a negative angle: throwing downward from a cliff. And try launching horizontally: a bullet fired horizontally and a
bullet dropped from the same height hit the ground at the same moment.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The vertical motion is $y = h + u \sin\theta \, t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2$. Setting $y = 0$ gives a quadratic whose
positive root is the flight time,

$$
T = \frac{u \sin\theta + \sqrt{u^2 \sin^2\theta + 2 g h}}{g},
$$

and the range is $u \cos\theta \, T$. Energy conservation gives the landing speed $\sqrt{u^2 + 2 g h}$ regardless of angle.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The cliff is a static polygon and the ground a half-plane. Both are contacts the engine handles the same way.
</div>
</Scenario>
