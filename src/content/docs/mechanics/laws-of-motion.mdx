## Block on a rough incline

<Scenario id="incline-friction">
<div slot="intuition">
Put a block on a gentle slope and friction holds it still. Tilt the slope more and at some angle it lets go.
That angle depends only on the roughness, not on the mass: a heavy block and a light one slip at the same tilt.
Once it slides, kinetic friction is a little weaker than the static friction that was holding it, so it does not just
creep, it accelerates. Drag the angle slider past the critical value and watch the free-body diagram switch.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Along the slope gravity contributes $m g \sin\theta$; the normal force is $N = m g \cos\theta$. At rest, static friction
equals $m g \sin\theta$ and cannot exceed $\mu_s N$, so the block stays until

$$
\tan\theta = \mu_s .
$$

Sliding, kinetic friction is $\mu_k N$ and the acceleration is $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)$.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
Friction is decided per contact point every step the way a student is taught: compute the force that would hold the
block, compare it with $\mu_s N$, and if it is too much, switch to $\mu_k N$ opposing the motion. The engine reports
the actual friction force, so at rest the free-body diagram shows $m g \sin\theta$, not $\mu_s N$.
Validated to 1e-10 in both regimes.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Pushing a block on a rough floor

<Scenario id="pushed-block">
<div slot="intuition">
Push gently and nothing happens, yet something is happening: friction is pushing back with exactly your force.
Push harder and friction grows to match, until it cannot. Then the block moves, and because kinetic friction is weaker
than the maximum static friction, it accelerates the moment it starts.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
The block moves when $F > \mu_s m g$. Once moving,

$$
a = \frac{F - \mu_k m g}{m} .
$$

</div>
<div slot="engine">
Below the threshold the engine's friction readout equals the push to 1e-10, which is the free-body diagram textbooks
draw. The slip is reported as a `friction_slip` event.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Atwood machine

<Scenario id="atwood">
<div slot="intuition">
Two masses hang on a string over a pulley. If they are equal, nothing moves. Make one heavier and it descends while
the other rises, both with the same acceleration, because the string keeps them moving together. The string's
tension is the same on both sides and lies between the two weights: more than the lighter weight (it is being pulled
up) and less than the heavier (it is being let down).
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Newton's law on each mass with a common acceleration $a$ and tension $T$:

$$
a = \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\, g, \qquad T = \frac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\, g .
$$

</div>
<div slot="engine">
The string is a rope constraint: an inequality on the path length over the pulley's tangent geometry. The engine
solves for the tension that keeps the path length fixed, and reports it. Validated to round-off against both formulas.
Set the masses equal and the acceleration readout is exactly zero.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Block on a table and a hanging mass

<Scenario id="table-and-hanging-mass">
<div slot="intuition">
A block on a table is tied to a mass hanging over the edge. The hanging weight pulls the whole system, but it has to
accelerate the block as well as itself, so it falls more slowly than free fall. Add table friction and there is a point
where the hanging weight is not enough to get the block moving at all.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With table friction $\mu$, the system moves if $m_2 g > \mu m_1 g$, with

$$
a = \frac{m_2 - \mu m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\, g, \qquad T = m_1 (a + \mu g) .
$$

The normal force on the block is $m_1 g$ regardless.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The table is a static polygon with friction, the rope bends over a pulley at its edge. After the hanging mass lands
on the floor the rope goes slack, which the event log reports, and a frictionless block simply keeps sliding.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Two blocks joined by a string

<Scenario id="connected-blocks">
<div slot="intuition">
Pull the front block and the string drags the other along. Both move together, so they share one acceleration, set by
your pull and their total mass. The string, though, only has to move the block behind it, so its tension is less than
your pull: the fraction of the total mass that it is hauling.

Now add floor friction. The acceleration drops, but watch the tension: it does not change at all. Friction takes from
each block in proportion to its mass, exactly as the pull is shared.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
For the pair, $F - \mu(m_1 + m_2)g = (m_1 + m_2)\,a$. For the block behind alone, $T - \mu m_1 g = m_1 a$. Together:

$$
a = \frac{F}{m_1 + m_2} - \mu g, \qquad T = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\, F .
$$

</div>
<div slot="engine">
The string is a rope constraint between two points on the blocks, and its tension is whatever keeps its length fixed.
The answer agrees to eight figures with and without friction. If the pull is too small to move the blocks, the
tension is not fixed by the laws of motion alone (either block's friction could be holding), and no value is claimed.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Apparent weight in a lift

<Scenario id="lift">
<div slot="intuition">
You feel heavier when a lift starts upward and lighter when it starts down. Your weight, the pull of the Earth, has
not changed. What you feel, and what a bathroom scale reads, is the push of the floor on your feet, and the floor
must push harder than your weight to accelerate you upward and less hard to let you accelerate down.

Slide the acceleration to −9.8 m/s², the lift in free fall. The floor's push drops to zero: you and the lift fall
together, and a scale under you reads nothing. That is weightlessness, and it is what astronauts in orbit feel.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
With the lift accelerating upward at $a$, the forces on the person are the floor's push $N$ up and $mg$ down:

$$
N - mg = ma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = m\,(g + a) .
$$

A scale shows $N / g$ kilograms.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The lift's floor is a body on a vertical track, hauled by a cable with exactly the force that gives floor and
passenger the chosen acceleration. The person simply stands on it. The normal force in the free-body diagram is then
the apparent weight, found by the contact solver and not put in: it matches $m(g+a)$ to a millionth of a newton,
including zero in free fall.
</div>
</Scenario>

## Recoil of a gun

<Scenario id="recoil">
<div slot="intuition">
When the charge fires, it pushes the shell forward and the gun backward with equal forces, for the same length of
time. Equal force for equal time means equal momentum, in opposite directions: the total was zero before and is zero
after. The gun is heavier, so it moves more slowly.

Energy is not shared so evenly. The light shell, moving fast, takes nearly all of it. That is why the shell is
dangerous and the recoil is only a shove.
</div>
<div slot="maths">
Momentum is conserved, starting from rest:

$$
M V = m v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = \frac{m}{M}\, v .
$$

The kinetic energies are in the inverse ratio of the masses, so the shell carries a fraction $M / (M + m)$ of the
energy released.
</div>
<div slot="engine">
The charge is a pair of equal and opposite forces that act for two hundredths of a second: Newton's third law, as it
is. How long they act does not matter, only the impulse. Afterwards the total momentum is zero to round-off, and the
energy tab shows the work done by the charge equal to the kinetic energy of the two together.
</div>
</Scenario>
